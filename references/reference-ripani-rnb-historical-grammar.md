# The New Blue Music: Changes in Rhythm & Blues, 1950–1999 — Richard J. Ripani
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: 284 PDF pages | **Sections**: 8 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: technical

University Press of Mississippi, 2006, first edition. Eight chapters and methodological appendices; printed pages are eighteen pages behind the inspected PDF pages. The book offers a historical argument through **1999**. It does not establish a timeless R&B definition or independently explain twenty-first-century alternative R&B. Source notation is selectively checked; this reference is not a transcription collection.

## Mental Model (read first)

R&B changes through varying combinations of melody, harmony, rhythm, form, vocal practice and production, with relations to blues, gospel, jazz, soul, funk, pop and rap. Ripani calls their wider shared musical resources the **blues system** and the broader continuum **new blue music**. Neither means that every recording contains the same features, or that all music using one feature belongs to one genre.

His main empirical study uses 125 recordings: the twenty-five leading Billboard-derived R&B hits of each decade from 1950 through 1999, ranked through Joel Whitburn. This is a deliberately selected hit sample, not a census or a representative random sample of all R&B. Keep its measurements, the author's historical interpretations and judgments of musical value separate.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapter 1: Category, core style and historical evidence

Ripani treats R&B as a broad **trade category** containing multiple styles rather than one stable genre. Chart inclusion and musical style can diverge. His examples of stylistically atypical chart hits show why a record's category placement cannot be read directly from its notes, and why one blues-related feature cannot prove an R&B identity.

His **core style** is an attempt to identify recurring musical relations within that changing category. It inherits resources from earlier African American musical practices while incorporating other traditions. Use this to ask historically specific questions, not to rank racial or cultural purity. Brackett/Lena add the institutional, labeling and community processes through which categories work; Ripani adds a focused account of musical change. These explanations complement one another without becoming interchangeable.

Separate recorded/release versions, dates, charts and analytical features. A connection by shared musical grammar is not proof that one artist heard or influenced another. A feature can participate in an R&B lineage while also belonging to gospel, blues, jazz, country or pop practice.

*Locators: chapter 1, especially pp. 5–12; chapter 8, “R&B as a Collection of Styles and Genres.”*

### Chapter 2: Blues mode, pitch areas and melodic practice

**Blues system** names a pool of musical resources used in varying combinations across traditions. The **blues mode** is not adequately represented as a fixed major scale with a few wrong notes. Ripani treats characteristic thirds, sevenths and the more disputed fifth region as variable pitch areas whose behavior depends on context. Bends, slides, contour and placement matter as well as pitch membership.

Distinguish a **blue third** as a flexible region from an equal-tempered minor third, and both from an ordinary minor-mode melody. A fixed-pitch transcription can only approximate continuously inflected singing or playing. The figure called a blues scale is a model, not a uniquely correct exhaustive pitch inventory. Exact intonation claims require suitable evidence.

The **flatted fifth / sharp fourth** has a less settled status. Ripani often hears it moving toward the fifth or fourth rather than functioning like the third or seventh, but acknowledges counterexamples and a more sustained accented use in jazz. Preserve that qualification rather than teaching a mandatory resolution. His African-origin and harmonic-series explanations are attributed historical/theoretical arguments, not proof of an innate racial faculty or one origin for every use.

Melodic practice also includes ornaments, recurring figures and phrasing transmitted through listening. The same pitch collection with different contour, emphasis and delivery can belong to a different musical situation.

*Locators: chapter 2, “Mode” and melodic discussion, pp. 17–34, especially pp. 20–26.*

### Chapter 2: Harmony and melody can operate on different terms

A chord's construction does not dictate its function. A **dominant-seventh-type sonority** can operate as tonic in blues-system practice; it need not demand resolution down a fifth. Likewise, a shift between static harmonic levels can mark a section without behaving as a conventional dominant-to-tonic progression. Functional progressions also occur: the point is mixture and context, not a ban on functional hearing.

Thirdless accompaniment can leave room for a voice's flexible third. A blues-inflected melodic line can coexist with chord tones that a strictly chord-matching account would consider contradictory. Describe this relation before diagnosing error. Harmonic parallelism and sustained harmonic fields need not be failures of voice leading.

Use Laitz when tonal function is supported, and retain Cook's repertoire-sensitive method choice. Do not force a tonic seventh into a secondary-dominant explanation merely because of its spelling. Conversely, do not call every seventh chord “blues” or treat every R&B progression as nonfunctional. The voice, bass, surrounding chords, phrase and historical practice help decide.

*Locator: chapter 2, “Harmony,” pp. 34–42. Ripani's broad contrast with European practice is an explanatory framing, not an exhaustive account of either tradition.*

### Chapter 2: Forms and cycles

**Twelve-bar blues** is a flexible three-phrase family in Ripani's account, especially in folk practice; twelve measured bars are common but not universally required. Vocal statements and instrumental responses can affect the actual span. A literal bar count alone cannot settle function or lineage.

Gospel-related song forms and harmonic patterns contribute another route into R&B. These relations include responsorial organization and sectional development, not only impassioned tone. Distinguish a musical relation to gospel from an assertion about a performer's religion or biography.

**Cyclic form** is Ripani's preferred term for repeating musical units, usually one to four measures, often composed of riffs. It can involve static harmony **or a short repeating progression**. It often supports a soloist, call-and-response and an increase of intensity, but these are common properties rather than necessary conditions. A cycle can occupy a section or most of a track.

Ask what changes while the cycle persists: vocal density and inflection, articulation, timbre, layering, register, responses or recorded emphasis. Harmonic stasis need not mean formal inactivity. Cyclic form predates digital sampling; a repeated part alone does not prove a sampled loop. Its explanatory work also differs from Caplin's Classical formal functions.

*Locator: chapter 2, “Form,” pp. 42–45.*

### Chapter 2: Rhythm, voice and improvisation

**Offbeat phrasing** and layered rhythmic relations need a stated pulse or reference, not merely a claim of syncopation. Ripani uses **swing** broadly for intentional rhythmic “at-oddness” with the pulse, including elastic or rubato phrasing. His **triplet swing** is a narrower subdivision concept. The statistical absence of triplet swing does not mean the absence of all expressive timing or compelling groove.

**Half-swing** names an intermediate feel in his discussion with James Brown. A notated approximation does not prove that the performer mentally calculated that ratio. Distinguish subdivision, accent, relative placement, the backing's tempo and vocal elasticity. Danielsen supplies the broader account of sounding gesture and embodied groove; Ohriner supplies finer distinctions for rapped delivery. Their terminology should not be silently equated with Ripani's broad use of swing.

Vocal resources include **melisma** (several notes on one syllable), rubato, vibrato, glissando, grain or rough timbre, falsetto, declamation and spoken delivery. Identify their particular contour, timing, location and relation to accompaniment when evidence allows. No one item is exclusive to R&B, and “soulful” is not a sufficient description.

**Improvisation** uses learned stylistic resources and recombination, including altered pitches, phrasing, timbre or words. It is not invention without preparation. Berliner/Monson deepen the account of situated choices and interaction; a recorded ornament does not by itself prove spontaneous invention in that take.

*Locators: chapter 2, pp. 46–60, especially pp. 52–57; Appendix C for the Brown interview.*

### Chapters 3–4: 1950s variety and 1960s soul

The 1950s setting includes jump-derived dance music, blues, vocal-group styles and gospel-related developments. In Ripani's account, Louis Jordan's jump practice combines blues form, backbeat emphasis, jazz inflection and dance function. Gospel-related forms and vocal practices enter the developing R&B language through more than a change of lyrical subject. Do not collapse the decade into one instrumentation or one feel.

The 1960s account relates soul to continuing gospel resources, changing ensembles and commercial institutions. It complicates a simple “Motown equals diluted pop” story by identifying gospel-related records and their R&B reception. Historical crossover, stylistic mixture and artistic merit remain different claims.

A concrete change links arrangement and vocal practice: Ripani describes electric bass taking over chord-root support that had been prominent in the doo-wop bass voice. The bass singer can then become the lowest member of a more integrated backing-vocal harmony. This is a functional redistribution, not simply a new instrument added to an otherwise unchanged genre.

The rise of cyclic organization alongside reduced use of twelve-bar patterns does not mean that blues-system resources disappear. Ask which resources persist, which alter function and which traditions become more consequential in the specific recording.

*Locators: chapter 3, pp. 62–80; chapter 4, especially pp. 81–84; chapter 8's comparison of the decades.*

### Chapters 5–6: 1970s and 1980s coexistence

Funk's cyclic, interlocking rhythmic organization, disco's dance uses, jazz-related harmonic resources and expanding electronics coexist in the 1970s account. The sample's increased chord vocabulary and increased cyclic form need not conflict: some recordings use sparse repeated harmony while others broaden the harmonic inventory. An average can conceal this variety. Avoid a linear story of ever greater harmonic sophistication or inevitable decline.

The 1980s sustain earlier artists and practices alongside new technologies and stylistic combinations. Synthesizers, drum machines and sequencing change timbral roles and construction possibilities; the recorded outcome still needs a musical account. The hardware's availability alone does not explain which choices a producer made.

Ripani's 1980s sample shows both more chord types and more cyclic organization; a cycle can contain a multi-chord progression. The emergence of **new jack swing** links machine-shaped rhythmic subdivisions, R&B vocal/sectional practices and a rap-related production sensibility. Specify the rhythmic level and the recording's combination rather than identifying it solely by a singer or a machine.

These are overlapping tendencies, not a decade-by-decade replacement of every older practice. Moore/Zak clarify how arrangement and recorded presentation interact with the history; Danielsen clarifies what the repeated layers do.

*Locators: chapters 5–6, pp. 102–148, especially pp. 123–124, 130–131 and 145–148.*

### Chapter 7: 1990s mixtures and the limits of averages

Rap, contemporary R&B, new jack swing, neo-soul and other labels overlap in Ripani's account rather than composing a single uniform sound. Sampling, newly generated sounds, rapped and sung sections, gospel-related melisma, funk resources and polished recorded presentation can coexist in different proportions.

The decade's hit sample has slower average tempos, fewer chord types than the 1980s sample and a greater share of cyclic form. These measurements do not establish that all 1990s R&B became slower or harmonically simpler, and fewer chords do not mean less composition. “Rhythm Nation” supplies one of his examples of interacting loop, rhythm and vocal practices; “Poison” supplies another, with a different combination.

A critical exception: none of the decade's selected twenty-five hits comes from 1990 or 1991. Ripani therefore supplements the decade-wide view with annual number-one records to examine early-1990s triplet swing and its later decline. This shows why a decade mean can miss a short-lived but influential practice. Similarly, reduced counted blue notes in a sample containing more rapped vocals need not mean a proportional loss of historical connection.

Do not extend these findings unaided into 2000–present R&B. Use them to formulate historical comparisons, then obtain appropriate later evidence for later artists, practices and category uses.

*Locator: chapter 7, pp. 149–169, especially pp. 152–154 and 165–169.*

### Chapter 8 and Appendix A: What the historical argument can establish

Ripani interprets the changes as persistence and renewal of blues-system resources, contesting a simple narrative in which crossover erases the tradition. He also argues for **ambiguity** across pitch, rhythm and delivery as a central aesthetic, and interprets some changes as **re-Africanization**. Preserve these as his claims, alongside his engagement with George, Baraka and Waterman. Feature counts do not independently prove cultural authenticity, artist intention, commercial causation or the emotional validity of a whole community's music.

The book's broad African/European contrasts and some identity-based authenticity judgments require critical distance. Historically learned practices and institutions can be analyzed without making inherited race a musical qualification or imposing a purity ranking. Brackett/Lena help investigate category and community; Frith/Wilson keep the critic's evaluative standards open to examination.

**Measurement boundaries** matter when citing the study:

- The 125-record sample comes from chart rankings, with additional illustrative records outside the statistical sample. Do not mix their denominators.
- “Chord types” counts root/triadic-quality distinctions, including suspended-fourth structures, but does not count sixths, sevenths, ninths, elevenths and thirteenths as additional types. It is not a full measure of harmonic richness, voicing or voice leading.
- I/IV/V/other categories collapse some chord-quality differences; the “passing” category also contains very brief changes and chordless rhythmic sections. The category name is not a tonal-function diagnosis.
- Blue-note presence is an ordinal rating, not a precise intonation measurement.
- Triplet swing is distinguished from the broader expressive-timing category. A measurement convention must not become a universal definition.
- Radio/retail versions are preferred where available; different versions may change the analytical object.

*Locators: chapter 8, pp. 170–192, especially pp. 186–190; Appendix A, pp. 193–198. The chord-count definitions on printed p. 193 / PDF p. 211 were visually checked.*

## Worked Example

Ripani's **“Poison” by Bell Biv DeVoe** example combines a triplet-swing snare subdivision, driving bass, sparse production and a dance tempo in his account of new jack swing. The result connects R&B's historical vocal/rhythmic field with a particular late-1980s/early-1990s production language. “Soulful singing” alone would miss the interaction that identifies the connection.

His caption explicitly leaves unresolved whether the part labeled synthesizer was played for the recording or sampled from an earlier source. Preserve that uncertainty: a sound's musical role is describable without knowing its production provenance. ## Decision Rules & Judgment

1. Date and identify the recording/version before calling it R&B. Separate its musical relations from historical trade labels and community membership.
2. Explain the interaction of voice, rhythmic reference, harmony, form and recorded presentation; identify adjacent traditions sharing those resources.
3. Treat blues pitch as contextual and potentially flexible. Do not substitute an equal-tempered scale formula for an unheard performance.
4. Distinguish sonority from function, and cyclic repetition from absence of formal activity. A tonic seventh or repeated progression needs a repertoire-appropriate explanation.
5. Keep broad swing, triplet subdivision, rubato, meter and groove distinct. No single timing ratio defines the whole tradition.
6. When citing a trend, state the sample and measurement. Check whether aggregated data hide contrasting practices or underrepresent an early/late period.
7. Preserve Ripani's historical thesis without turning it into a purity test or a proof of aesthetic decline or progress.
8. Stop the source's direct historical authority at 1999. Later cases require later evidence; continuity is a question to investigate.
9. Neither feature resemblance nor a genre label proves influence, sample identity, aesthetic success or predicted liking.

## Key Takeaways

- R&B is a changing historical field, not pop plus a vocal ornament.
- Blues-system resources operate through combinations and contextual functions.
- Harmony, rhythm, voice, form and production must be heard in relation.
- A chart category, a musical lineage and an aesthetic verdict are different objects.
- The study's dates, sample and measurement choices belong with its conclusions.
