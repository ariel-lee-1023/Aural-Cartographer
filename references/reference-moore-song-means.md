# Song Means: Analysing and Interpreting Recorded Popular Song — Allan F. Moore

## Mental Model (read first)
A recorded song offers an encounter with a performed persona in a constructed sound-world. Analysis earns its place by explaining that encounter: identify audible relationships, compare them with relevant norms, and show how they support an interpretation. Meaning is neither a code with one correct translation nor something a listener can assert without constraints. The recording, the song abstracted from it, and the artist's biography are different objects of inquiry.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1. Methodology: from a track to a defensible interpretation
**Core idea:** Ask what makes this particular track intelligible and consequential to a listener. A score cannot exhaust a recording, and a label cannot replace listening. Start with observations, move to relationships, then interpret; return to the observations to test whether another interpretation fits better. The choice of an interpretive question determines which analytical details matter.

Keep analysis of a sounding configuration separate from claims about an originator's intention. Intentions may enter through interviews or session documentation; they cannot simply be read out of a chord or voice. Moore's listener-oriented position gives intention less authority than some alternative interpretive approaches. It does not make every interpretation equally well supported. *Locator: ch. 1; ch. 7, “Intention.”*

### 2. Shape: functional layers, soundbox, timbre
**Functional layers** distinguish musical jobs rather than fixed instruments:
- **Explicit beat layer:** articulates a pattern of beats, commonly with nominally unpitched percussion.
- **Functional bass layer:** provides a low structural foundation and often connects harmonic roots while contributing to groove.
- **Melodic layer:** presents principal melodies; lead voice and instruments can exchange this function.
- **Harmonic filler layer:** occupies the registral space between bass and melodic layers; its instrumentation often strongly cues style.

An instrument can perform several jobs, a layer can be absent, and a departure can become expressive. Map entries, withdrawals and transfers of function before calling an arrangement “busy” or “minimal.” These are descriptive norms, not a four-part prescription. *Locator: ch. 2, “Texture: Layers.”*

The **soundbox** models perceived recorded space: lateral position, vertical/register placement, and proximity/depth. It concerns the illusion experienced by a listener, not a reconstruction of where musicians physically stood. Inspect centrality, spread, foreground/background, and changes over time. Moore describes **diagonal**, **cluster** and **triangular** mixes as historically useful configurations; do not treat them as timeless templates. Timbre and the distribution of spectral energy help constitute content rather than merely decorate notes. No soundbox can be drawn reliably from lyrics or album metadata alone. *Locator: ch. 2, “Texture: Soundbox” and “Timbre.”*

### 3. Form: time and harmony working together
Distinguish beat, metre, hypermetre, phrase grouping and syncopation. A drum groove alone does not establish the whole temporal organization; bass, melody and harmonic changes can reinforce or resist it. Track whether a repeated unit changes function at a formal boundary.

Moore's **modal theory** resists treating all popular harmony as defective common-practice tonality. Identify the tonal centre, pitch collection and locally effective relationships before assigning functional labels. Distinguish **loops**, which renew a harmonic cycle, from **periods**, whose directed phrase relationships articulate closure. A static bass does not mean no harmonic or expressive movement. Use an actual transcription or supplied analysis when naming chords; damaged converted notation is insufficient. *Locator: ch. 3, “Time,” “Harmony: Modal Theory,” “Loops,” “Periods.”*

### 4. Delivery: contour, voice and words
Melodic contour and phrase structure interact with stress, breath, register and articulation. The same written words can invite different readings when the singer delays a syllable, changes vocal grain or makes a phrase difficult to complete. Describe the relevant feature and what changes across the passage, rather than translating vocal roughness automatically into honesty.

Lyrics contribute narratives and address, but their written form cannot settle the recording's meaning. Investigate the relation between what is said and how the voice delivers it: reinforcement, qualification, exaggeration, irony or unresolved tension. Do not invent an irony merely because accompaniment and words have different affective associations. *Locator: ch. 4, “Melody,” “The Voice,” “Lyrics.”*

### 5. Style: establish the appropriate comparison
Style supplies norms against which a detail becomes ordinary, conspicuous or disruptive. Compare the track with a justified local practice and historical moment. The book's chronological survey provides examples from pre-war repertories through later popular styles; it is not an exhaustive genre dictionary. Use a style claim to generate a listening question, then test it against the actual recording. *Locator: ch. 5.*

### 6. Friction: expectation against realization
**Textural friction** occurs when an established or anticipated relationship between functional layers is disturbed. **Formal friction** concerns resistance to expected formal relationships. The useful question is not just whether something is unusual but which expectation makes it unusual and what the difference does in this track.

First establish the norm from the track and relevant style; identify the departure; trace whether it is sustained, resolved or transformed; relate its timing to voice and narrative. Friction can strengthen a work or be incidental. It does not automatically signal sophistication. *Locator: ch. 6.*

### 7. Persona: who meets whom in this recording?
The **persona** is a performed identity inferred through the track. It need not be the singer's private self or the lyric's protagonist. A listener can occupy the position of **protagonist**, **antagonist** or **observer**; the lyrics' “you” need not be the actual listener.

The **personic environment** is the musical setting in relation to that persona. Ask whether accompaniment supports, frames, resists or unsettles the voice, and how those relations develop. These are interpretive alternatives, not automatic translations from a timbre to an emotion.

**Proxemics** concerns intimate, personal/private, social and public relations of distance. Apparent proximity depends on more than loudness: reverberation, delivery, register, texture and the persona's fit with its environment contribute. A dry close vocal may invite intimacy, intrusion or vulnerability depending on the encounter. Do not equate less reverb with greater sincerity. *Locator: ch. 7, “Theory,” “Address,” “Persona/Personic Environment.”*

### 8. Reference: how sound can mean beyond itself
Semiotic readings need an account of the link between sound and referent. **Conceptual metaphor** and **image schema** describe how bodily experience can organize understanding—for example, experienced movement, force, containment or direction. **Ecological perception** emphasizes what sounds disclose about possible actions and environments.

Use these approaches when an embodied description illuminates an audible relation. Explain what supports the metaphor and where another listener might differ. Neither supplies a universal dictionary in which an ascending line always means aspiration. *Locator: ch. 8, “Semiotics,” “Embodied Cognition,” “Ecological Perception.”*

### 9. Belonging: authenticity and intertextuality
Ask **who is authenticated**, rather than whether the recording contains an objective substance called authenticity:
- **First person / authenticity of expression:** the performance makes the musician's expression credible.
- **Second person / authenticity of experience:** it validates or articulates the audience's experience.
- **Third person / authenticity of execution:** it credibly articulates the experience or practice of absent others, including a tradition.

These are different possible achievements. Studio construction, borrowing or a cover version cannot by themselves disqualify them. **Intertextuality** opens a recording to other texts and practices; recognize a resemblance before claiming quotation, and document contact before claiming direct influence. *Locator: ch. 9.*

### 10–11. Syntheses and questions: selective integration
A full reading selects the relationships that explain the track, rather than mechanically reporting every analytical category. The final questions turn the method into prompts for hearing and interpretation. For Aural-Cartographer, the operational sequence is: identify version and evidence, sketch the significant relations, propose an interpretation, state its strongest rival, then justify an evaluation separately. This sequence is a project synthesis, not a named Moore framework. *Locator: chs. 10–11.*

## Worked Example
In Moore's reading of Roy Orbison's “Oh Pretty Woman,” formal events are interpreted alongside the protagonist's changing situation. The verses move from seeing the woman to attempting an encounter; the extended bridge temporarily inhabits an imagined successful exchange; the return confronts failure before the final reversal. The recurring harmonic material and guitar hook help make continuity and transformation perceptible. The lesson is to explain how musical timing participates in the narrative, not to retell lyrics and append chord names. This is a reconstruction of Moore's analysis, not a claim of fresh listening. *Locator: ch. 10, “Pretty Woman,” printed pp. 316–319.*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When “production” seems incidental, test whether changing proximity, timbre or layer balance would change the persona encounter.
- When analysis starts with genre, identify which expectation the label supplies and verify it locally.
- When a user dislikes a voice, distinguish delivery, apparent proximity, persona and surrounding arrangement before inferring a dislike of the singer.
- When two versions share a song, treat their tracks as separate interpretive objects.
- When a claim concerns intention, require evidence outside the track and keep it distinct from the listener's reading.
- When no audio or trustworthy sonic analysis is available, present the method or a conditional interpretation; do not supply fabricated details.
- When a formal irregularity is found, explain its effect before praising its complexity.

## Key Takeaways
1. Functions and relationships explain more than instrument lists.
2. Recorded space helps constitute meaning.
3. Persona, singer, protagonist and listener position must remain distinguishable.
4. Interpretation is constrained, plural and revisable.
5. Authentication concerns an achievement in a relationship, not the absence of mediation.
