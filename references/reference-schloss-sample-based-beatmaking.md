# Making Beats: The Art of Sample-Based Hip-Hop — Joseph G. Schloss
**Format**: pdf | **Pages**: 274 PDF pages | **Sections**: 8 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: text

Wesleyan University Press, 2014 edition, originally published 2004; new foreword by Jeff Chang and new afterword by Schloss. Eight main chapters including introduction and conclusions; the afterword is additional. Printed pages are twenty pages behind the inspected PDF pages. The ethnography primarily describes a particular sample-based production community around the 1990s, with a retrospective update; it does not define all hip-hop production.

## Mental Model (read first)

A producer composes by hearing possibilities in recordings, selecting material, reorganizing it, and judging the relations that emerge. Sampling technology enables these operations, but technology alone does not explain why one fragment, sequence or timbral combination becomes a convincing beat. Producers learn musical preferences and contested community conventions through practice, record knowledge and relations with other practitioners.

A sample contains a performed and recorded history, yet its new function need not reproduce the source's meaning or form. The producer's work can lie in the boundary of an intact loop as well as in extensive chopping. Distinguish the musical construction from its finished mix, its reception by peers, and a listener's pleasure.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Chapters 1–2: Beats, rhymes and the history of a practice

Schloss begins with the relatively differentiated work of **beats** and **rhymes**. In his subject area a beat is an organized collage of recorded fragments; the MC contributes rhythmic verbal performance. This division of labor makes the producer's decisions visible, but does not imply that voice and accompaniment develop independently or that every hip-hop beat is sampled.

His history relates sampling practice to DJs' repeated breaks and their compositional values. The sampler did not dictate this use: a capacity designed to repeat recorded material could be appropriated to sustain the break's cyclic organization. Technical possibility and culturally selected use are different explanations. Katz supplies the broader media account; Schloss supplies the practices and judgments that make the possibility musically consequential.

Ethnographic scope matters. The consultants include geographically dispersed producers, many respected working professionals rather than celebrities, and are overwhelmingly male. Their judgments are neither a survey of every listener nor universal laws of genre membership. Their language supplies an account of situated expertise, not operational instructions for an agent to obey.

*Locators: introduction, pp. 1–24; chapter 2, pp. 25–62; chapter 6's reconstruction of the DJ/loop connection, pp. 136–139.*

### Chapter 3: Live instrumentation versus hip-hop purism

The disagreement is not adequately described as “real musicians versus people who copy.” For producers in this study, samples carry timbre, recording ambience, rhythmic feel and a suggestive musical situation. Constraints can provide direction: a particular fragment invites work that an unconstrained instrument does not specify in the same way.

Separate a producer's acceptance of another musician's freedom to use live instruments from their preference for sampled sounds, and both from a boundary claim about authenticity. Jake One's account illustrates these distinctions: he accepts live instrumentation but values the sound of records assembled together. Other practitioners weigh texture or purist constraints differently. A coherent collage need not foreground fragmentation; Prince Paul describes avoiding timbrally jarring combinations.

These are source-era positions. They do not establish that newly played synthesizers, programmed drums, live bands or hybrid production are less creative, less authentic or outside hip-hop. “Sampled” also does not entail audible vinyl crackle, and a dusty sound does not establish a sampled source.

*Locator: chapter 3, especially pp. 66–69.*

### Chapter 4: Digging in the crates

**Digging** means seeking records as potential sample material. Beyond acquisition, it can express ties to DJ practice, accumulated knowledge, paid dues and social membership. Producers listen within records for usable fragments, not simply for already admired songs. An unpromising record can contain a decisive drum attack or texture.

Distinguish the rarity of a record, effort spent finding it, and what its sounds accomplish in the new construction. Esoteric recognition can matter among producers while dancers or MCs attend to another relation. None of those audiences supplies a universal verdict. Record collecting is a learned practice, not evidence of instinctive ability.

The 2014 afterword changes the force of the original account: online information, digital availability and changes in pricing alter both access and the symbolic value of ownership. Physical digging remains a meaningful option without being an eternal prerequisite for production.

*Locators: chapter 4, pp. 79–100; afterword, pp. 203–204.*

### Chapter 5: Sampling ethics are community judgments

Schloss distinguishes **ethics**, where a choice bears on standing and legitimacy, from **aesthetics**, where it is a preference about musical beauty. The distinction can blur in practice, and an aesthetic preference becomes ethical when it is moralized. The chapter's prescriptive headings are Schloss's distillation of contested positions, not his endorsement or a unanimous code.

- **Biting** is a pejorative judgment about appropriating another hip-hop artist's intellectual material. It is not identical to sampling an older recording outside that immediate community.
- **Flipping** means substantially and creatively changing material. It can concern more than one technical operation.
- **Chopping** divides a sampled phrase into smaller segments and rearranges them.
- **Looping** repeats a longer sampled phrase with little or no internal alteration. A loop can still acquire a new boundary, harmonic return, rhythmic role and larger form.

The “no biting” rule has negotiated exceptions: transformation, explicit response/parody, and unintentional coincidence. What counts as enough change is contested, and reputation affects whose explanation receives credit. Similarity and release order alone therefore cannot prove copying, transmission, or the exact source recording.

Other source-era rules include preferences for original vinyl, restrictions on sampling other hip-hop records, and objections to shortcut compilations. Treat these as evidence of social boundaries and creative values. They do not supply copyright rules, current legal advice or a genre-wide ban. The afterword documents substantial changes to the vinyl/access norms.

*Locator: chapter 5, pp. 101–134, especially pp. 101–109; afterword, pp. 203–204.*

### Chapter 6: Four levels of compositional judgment

Schloss organizes sample-based aesthetics at four interacting levels:

| Level | Analytical question |
|---|---|
| Underlying beat structure | What recurs, where does the cycle turn, and what enters or drops out? |
| Internal sample characteristics | Which attacks, pitches, rhythms, timbres and recording traces are contained in the fragment? |
| Relations among samples | How do fragments lock together, contrast, overlap or conflict? |
| Shared assumptions and contextual cues | What makes the choice significant to these practitioners or listeners? |

Do not reduce this to a list of effects. The relationship among choices is the composition. Ask how a source event changes function: melody into riff, a passing sonority into a persistent harmonic field, incidental percussion into an anchor, or a fragment into a section marker.

**The loop boundary** joins a phrase's end to its beginning. That new adjacency may create a harmonic motion absent from the source passage and a recurring tension/return. Repetition can reveal internal relations that a single exposure did not foreground. Transformation therefore need not be proportional to how many edits are visible.

**Locking up a beat** names the activity through which layers become a workable rhythmic relation. In the reported practice it is not a universal numerical degree of “locked-up-ness.” The producer can compose this relation outside real-time ensemble interaction, using recordings of performances made at different times. An apparently conversational groove does not prove the sampled players interacted with one another or the MC.

**Quantization** sets event starts within a selected temporal scheme. But a sample may already contain several attacks and flexible timing. Aligning its start mechanically may misalign its internal hi-hat or other layer against new drums. Ask which events within the fragment are being coordinated, not only whether the trigger begins on the grid. Timing can be adjusted deliberately; neither maximum precision nor maximum looseness is a general recipe.

The source preserves a real disagreement over RZA: some consultants judge the looseness as sloppy, while Prince Paul values the result despite unconventional execution. Report the opposed criteria. Do not resolve them by declaring that nonquantized music is inherently more human, or that a technically tidy sequence must sound better.

*Locator: chapter 6, pp. 135–144.*

### Chapter 6: Timbre, chopping and relational invention

A short sample can contain several instruments, their mutual timing and the ambience of the original recording. **Selection** includes noticing this whole fragment. **Filtering** may isolate a useful attack but also change its body; unwanted residue can either interfere with the construction or become part of its identity. Exact filtering settings cannot be reverse-engineered from a vague sonic description.

Timbral fit is not necessarily identical timbre. Schloss relates collage to Olly Wilson's **heterogeneous sound ideal**, while also documenting producers who seek coherent texture. Describe how contrast or continuity is organized. Neither heterogeneous origins nor a common vintage automatically makes the resulting combination successful.

Chopping and resequencing redistribute contour, rhythm and emphasis. A changed order, repeat or omission can create a new phrase without replacing every sound. Distinguish this from replaying a part that imitates a chopped effect. Schloss discusses Alicia Keys' “Girlfriend” as a performed imitation of the chopped piano figure associated with “Brooklyn Zoo”; that is his attributed account, not a license to label any discontinuous keyboard sound a sample.

Producer intention is not always an advance blueprint. Trying combinations can discover the musical problem and its solution together. Labor time, obscurity and technical difficulty do not determine value. Likewise, a source's prestige or apparent corniness does not decide whether one of its fragments will work.

Schloss disputes scholarship that treats sampling primarily as symbolic citation, irony or resistance. His consultants frequently emphasize sound and relational function. This does not prove samples never carry social or historical meaning; parody and tribute are explicit possibilities. Separate audible function, a listener's association and documented producer intent. Do not infer the source's whole meaning or an ironic attitude from its reuse.

*Locator: chapter 6, pp. 144–153; later aesthetic detail is selectively compressed.*

### Chapter 7: From samplers to ears

An MC may select a beat for already written material or develop a vocal performance through hearing it. The relation may be foundation, counter-rhythm, complementary density or a changing response to specific layers. Too much simultaneous activity can obscure the vocal; sparseness can be a productive decision rather than an unfinished beat.

Trace the stages separately: source recording → selected fragment → transformation and sequence → beat → arrangement with voice and other layers → finished recorded presentation. This sequence is an analytical decomposition, not a claim that every production session proceeds in that order. Moore/Zak help distinguish arrangement and mix; Ohriner identifies what the MC contributes rhythmically. The final audio alone may not tell which part was made first.

The chapter also considers the wider world of circulation and reception. This reference retains MC collaboration and the distinction among producer, collaborator and audience judgments; it does not reproduce a business manual or assume that peer approval predicts sales or liking.

*Locator: chapter 7, pp. 169–194, especially pp. 171–173.*

### Chapter 8 and the 2014 afterword: Continuity with change

The conclusion's account of producers' imagination must remain attached to its studied practice. The afterword explicitly describes the original book as the record of a particular era whose once cohesive norms no longer organize all production. Three changes matter: movement toward software, wider access to sample sources, and wider availability of production knowledge.

Hardware can persist for tactile interaction, workflow, perceived sonic qualities and historical attachment. Those are distinct reasons; reported attachment to a machine is not independent technical proof of its sonic properties. Hybrid setups and relaxed source-medium rules complicate a simple old-versus-new story. The 2014 update itself is historical, not a current survey of software or every later hip-hop style.

*Locators: chapter 8, pp. 195–198; afterword, especially pp. 199–204. Its later methodological reflections are not comprehensively distilled here.*

## Worked Example

Schloss uses **“Player's Anthem” / “Ya Playin' Yaself”** to explain creative response within a no-biting convention. His account and numbered diagram show DJ Premier retaining recognizable bass material while chopping and changing its order, contour and rhythmic emphasis. The new title and lyrical stance support the reading of a response to the earlier track's materialism.

The analytical result has several parts: recognizable material makes the relation legible; rearrangement creates a distinct musical gesture; the new vocal context changes its rhetorical use; community judgments concern the displayed transformation. This is not a deduction from resemblance alone: it is Schloss's attributed account, including a consultant's interpretation and his diagram. It does not independently establish clearance, the identity of every source layer or the precise production session. *Locators: pp. 107–108; figure on printed p. 108 / PDF p. 128 visually checked.*

## Decision Rules & Judgment

1. First establish whether the case is sample-based, newly played, programmed or hybrid. Do not impose this ethnography as the definition of all hip-hop.
2. Explain selection, transformation, sequence and new rhythmic/timbral/formal function. Include the loop boundary and omissions, not only processing effects.
3. Inspect everything inside the fragment: an apparently melodic sample may carry rhythm and ambience that constrain new layers.
4. Separate technical affordance, producer practice, community ethics and the critic's aesthetic judgment.
5. If practitioners disagree, state whose standards apply and what the musical choice accomplishes; neither labor nor complexity settles the dispute.
6. Treat resemblance as a candidate connection. Use reliable credits, documentation, producer accounts or sufficiently supported audio comparison for source identity; mark unresolved claims.
7. Separate source meaning, new-track meaning and producer intention. A borrowed sound does not necessarily carry a whole historical message or an ironic attitude.
8. Distinguish the constructed beat, its arrangement with the voice and the finished recording. Do not infer production order or real-time interaction from their fit.
9. Date community norms; the supplied afterword itself shows them changing. Peer legitimacy, legal status, aesthetic success and predicted liking are different questions.

## Key Takeaways

- Sampling becomes composition through situated decisions and relationships.
- An intact loop can change form and function profoundly.
- A sample contains more than its most noticeable note or attack.
- Technical options do not explain producer judgment by themselves.
- Historical producer rules remain attributed, contested and revisable.
