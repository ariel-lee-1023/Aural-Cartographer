# Classical Form: A Theory of Formal Functions for the Instrumental Music of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven — William E. Caplin
**Format**: md, checked against local PDF | **Pages**: 320 PDF pages | **Sections**: 17 | **Depth**: study | **Type**: technical

Oxford University Press, 1998. Scope: the high Viennese Classical instrumental repertoire, approximately 1780–1810. Locators use printed chapter/page numbering.

## Mental Model (read first)

Form concerns what a passage does at a particular moment and hierarchical level: beginning, continuing, ending or framing another unit. Grouping structure says how spans are bounded; formal function specifies their role. A formal type such as sentence or sonata combines functions, while a process such as fragmentation can appear in different types. The theory's historical specificity is part of its explanatory power.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Part I, chapters 1–2: Function requires harmonic evidence

- **Grouping structure / formal function / formal type:** four measures describe duration; presentation describes a role; sentence describes a design. Keep these distinct. Motives are smaller melodic-rhythmic components; a basic idea normally occupies two measures in a normative eight-measure theme and may contain several motives.
- **Harmonic functions:** tonic, pre-dominant and dominant describe tonal roles. **Prolongational progressions** sustain a harmony; **cadential progressions** confirm a key at a goal; **sequential progressions** connect a patterned succession and can destabilize a key. Identify which progression governs a span before deciding its formal function.
- **Cadential progression versus cadence:** harmony supplies necessary conditions, but a cadence also acts as the ending of a formal process. A tonic return inside a presentation is not thereby an authentic cadence. A dominant arrival is not automatically a half cadence.
- **Authentic closure:** root-position cadential dominant to root-position tonic; a tonic upper-voice arrival gives a perfect authentic cadence (PAC), while a non-tonic upper-voice arrival can give an imperfect authentic cadence (IAC). Check actual bass, melody and ending function rather than inferring PAC from V–I symbols alone.
- **Half cadence (HC):** Caplin's goal is a root-position dominant triad, stable enough to end the progression. An unresolved seventh on V does not meet this specific requirement. Do not silently substitute a looser textbook use of “half cadence.” Source: chapter 2, pp. 27–29.
- **Expanded cadential progression (ECP):** enlargement of a cadential harmonic progression can support an entire phrase. Starting tonic and pre-dominant can vary or be omitted. An ECP is not simply a cadence made longer by a held final chord.

### Part II, chapters 3–5: Sentence, period and their alternatives

- **Presentation:** a basic idea plus its immediate repetition, usually over tonic prolongation. Repetition may be exact, a statement–response relation or sequential in appropriate contexts. Its purpose is to establish material and initiate an ongoing process; a cadence is not its normative ending.
- **Continuation:** fragmentation, faster harmonic rhythm, sequence and increased surface activity are characteristic indicators. They need not all occur together. **Fragmentation** reduces the size of units; **liquidation** progressively removes characteristic motivic features. A passage may do either without doing the other. Source: chapters 1 and 3, pp. 9–12, 35–48.
- **Cadential function:** the ending phase governed by a cadential progression. In a normative sentence, continuation and cadential functions commonly fuse within the second phrase. Do not require a separate four-bar unit for every function.
- **Continuation ⇒ cadential:** Caplin's notation marks retrospective reinterpretation: what initially promises continuation proves to be governed by an expanded cadential progression while retaining continuational features. Preserve what could be inferred at each moment; do not substitute the final label for the listener's entire temporal experience. Source: chapter 3, pp. 45–47.
- **Sentence:** presentation followed by continuation with cadential closure; eight measures is a norm, not a definition by bar count. Extensions, expansions, compression and interpolation must be justified from functional behavior. A 2+2+4 grouping with no tonal-functional evidence remains only a candidate.
- **Antecedent:** basic idea plus a contrasting idea, ending with comparatively weak cadential closure, often HC or IAC. **Consequent:** returns the opening idea and ends more conclusively. The consequent's stronger closure—not just a second four-bar phrase—supports period interpretation.
- **Period:** antecedent–consequent organization. Compare cadence, key, harmonic plan and return of material. A first phrase with no cadence may instead be a compound basic idea. A dominant sustained before the apparent boundary may be prolongational rather than cadential.
- **Hybrid themes:** antecedent + continuation; antecedent + cadential; compound basic idea + continuation; compound basic idea + consequent. A **compound basic idea** combines basic and contrasting ideas without the cadence required for an antecedent. These are positive descriptions of functional combinations, not defective sentences or periods. Source: chapter 5, pp. 59–63.
- **Compound themes:** larger sentences and periods build from phrase-sized units. State the analytical level; a whole theme's opening function is not identical to the local function of each constituent phrase.

### Chapters 6–7: Small ternary and small binary

- **Small ternary:** exposition establishes and closes a thematic unit; contrasting middle loosens the organization, often emphasizing dominant; recapitulation restores opening material with suitable home-key closure. These are functions, not merely letters A–B–A. A return may be shortened or reshaped.
- **Small binary:** two parts without the full functional return characteristic of small ternary. Compare opening material, tonal course and cadence, including what the second part does with earlier material. Repeats or a two-part page layout alone do not settle the type.
- **Tight-knit / loose:** tight-knit organization favors tonal stability, clear cadences, motivic unity, efficient functional expression and symmetrical groupings. Loose organization can involve modulation, cadential evasion, diverse material, ambiguity and phrase irregularity. These are relative bundles of features, not good/bad or simple/complex rankings. Source: chapter 1, pp. 17–19; chapters 6–8.

### Part III, chapters 8–12: Looser regions and the articulation of goals

- **Subordinate theme:** normally confirms the subordinate key through PAC; often uses looser sentential functions and cadential enlargement. “Second theme” should not imply exactly one contrasting melody. A group can contain multiple themes, and an IAC may occasion another attempt at stronger closure.
- **Deceptive cadence:** the cadential goal is redirected harmonically, commonly to VI. **Evaded cadence:** the event after the cadential dominant belongs perceptually to a new beginning rather than closing the preceding process, even if dominant harmony receives a resolution. **Abandoned cadence:** the cadential progression is given up before achieving its projected goal. Identify where and how the expected closure fails; these are not synonyms. Source: chapter 8, especially pp. 101–109.
- **One more time:** repetitions can renew the attempt to obtain a projected cadence. Ask what remains unfulfilled and what changes in the new attempt. Do not infer this strategy from repetition alone.
- **Closing section / codetta:** postcadential material reinforces an already achieved goal. Distinguish added confirmation from material still needed to secure the first PAC. **Standing on the dominant** similarly prolongs an established dominant after an HC; it is not itself another cadence.
- **Transition:** loosens main-theme stability and prepares the subordinate-theme region. It may modulate or remain in the home key; thematic novelty is optional. Its terminal dominant and the new theme's beginning can be separated, fused or ambiguous. A texture change alone cannot settle the boundary.
- **Development:** a large formal region with tonal instability and varied organization. **Pre-core/core technique** differentiates preparatory material from a core with model, sequential repetition and continuation. Not all developments have a core. Motivic development can also occur elsewhere; do not confuse a process with this large-scale section.
- **Retransition:** prepares a return, often with home-key dominant emphasis. Determine where return preparation becomes the governing role instead of assigning the label to any late passage of a development.
- **Recapitulation:** alters exposition functions to secure home-key resolution. Main theme, transition and subordinate theme can be recomposed, compressed, expanded or fused. Thematic return and tonal return may require separate boundaries.
- **Coda:** follows the essential recapitulation and can supply compensatory functions, revisit underdeveloped material or renew development before final closure. Distinguish it from the local closing section and from every passage heard after a returning tune.

### Part IV, chapters 13–17: Full-movement forms

- **Sonata:** main theme, transition and subordinate-theme functions organize exposition; development and recapitulation change their tonal and temporal situation. Cadential goals and key relations can outweigh contrasting thematic labels. Deviations require a stated norm and a positive account of their work.
- **Slow movements:** sonata, large ternary, sonata without development and theme-and-variations designs require different return/contrast questions. A slower tempo does not itself define a form.
- **Minuet/trio:** distinguish the organization within each dance from the da capo relationship between dances. Local small-form functions and whole-movement functions occupy different levels.
- **Rondo / sonata-rondo:** map refrain returns, episodes, tonal confirmation and any developmental or recapitulatory functions. Refrain recurrence alone does not explain the whole process.
- **Concerto:** opening ritornello and solo exposition are functionally different, not duplicate sonata expositions. Later ritornellos frame solo activity and tonal goals. A cadenza can greatly prolong a cadential dominant. Caplin explicitly distinguishes a closing ritornello from a genuine coda: returning orchestral framing need not have the coda's fresh formal context or loosening devices. Source: chapter 17, especially pp. 250–252.

## Worked Example

**Beethoven, Piano Sonata in F minor, Op. 2 No. 1, first movement, opening theme** (chapter 1, Example 1.1, pp. 9–11). Caplin hears a two-measure basic idea and its statement–response repetition as a presentation. The subsequent one-measure units fragment that size while harmonic rhythm accelerates; liquidation removes distinctive material as the phrase moves to its half cadence. Fragmentation and liquidation cooperate here but name different processes. The cadence closes this theme; it does not supply a PAC in the tonic.

The explanatory gain is temporal: establishment → increased activity → goal. Counting eight measures gives none of that. For a new passage, the same proposed reading must still be checked against tonic prolongation, unit sizes and the actual cadence. If only a verbal description is supplied, report the likely function and the missing evidence instead of asserting exact notes or a definitive boundary.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Establish a Classical stylistic context before treating these norms as binding. In pop, Romantic fragments or post-tonal repertoire, transfer a question cautiously rather than a compulsory form.
- Specify level, harmony and cadence before labeling a theme. Keep function, process and grouping separate.
- Explain a borderline case with two readings and the decisive evidence: a cadence, a tonic prolongation, a returning basic idea or a later reinterpretation.
- Do not equate lack of PAC with aesthetic failure. Suspension, fragmentation, open endings and cyclical continuation may belong to a different project.
- Formal analysis can suggest where urgency or closure might be heard. It does not prove actual perception, composer intention or merit.
- Compare score organization with performed articulation and recording. Tempo, breathing, voicing, edits and ambience can clarify or obscure the proposed boundaries.

## Key Takeaways

The useful question is not just “Which form is this?” but “What does this passage do now, what does it lead us to expect, and what changes our understanding of it?” Caplin answers within a delimited tonal style; keep that delimitation visible when crossing repertoires.
