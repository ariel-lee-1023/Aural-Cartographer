# The Passion for Music: A Sociology of Mediation — Antoine Hennion

## Mental Model (read first)
Music becomes present through mediators: bodies, instruments, notation, recordings, teachers, institutions, other listeners and practical arrangements. Taste is something people do, test and cultivate with responsive objects. Hennion refuses both the idea that an autonomous work simply causes pleasure and the idea that the work is only a screen for an already determined social identity.

## Frameworks & Structure
### Introduction: mediation without disappearance of the object
**Mediation** names operations through which objects, subjects and their relations take shape. A mediator is not merely a neutral pipe transmitting a finished musical substance. Nor is it necessarily an obstacle that must be removed to find real music.

The contrast between **linear** explanations, in which a given object causes a response, and **circular** explanations, in which a group encounters itself in its objects, is a problem to investigate. Simply adding “context” to an intrinsic musical effect leaves the problem intact. Trace the concrete operations that make the music available and effective. *Locator: Introduction, “The Object, Mediation, the Social” and “Why the Term ‘Mediation’?”*

### 1. Lasting Things: Durkheim and the status of objects
Hennion examines the tension in accounts of collective representation: material intermediaries may be acknowledged as necessary but then treated as passive signs of the group. Objects both help produce collective relations and are produced through them.

For analysis, do not decide in advance that an object is either an independent cause or a disguise for society. Ask what it enables participants to do and what difference its particular form makes. A recording's sound, a score's notation or an instrument's resistance cannot be removed from the account without testing what is lost. *Locator: ch. 1 and transition, “Restoring the Mediators.”*

### 2–5. Social readings, belief, art worlds and histories of art
These chapters examine efforts to connect works and social life: ideological readings, theories of belief, cooperative production, social history and the social within the artwork. Hennion's recurring question concerns what status each approach grants the object and the intermediaries it introduces.

**Critical reduction** becomes a problem when the analyst claims to explain attachment by exposing an underlying social cause while treating the listener's attention as illusion. Conversely, cataloguing institutions around an artwork does not yet explain the work's presence or effect. Follow how participants themselves attribute causes and evaluate differences.

The discussion of Bourdieu is not a license to deny class or domination. It challenges an explanatory asymmetry in which the observer knows the real causes and participants merely believe in objects. Cooperative networks similarly need to be examined for what their material products do, not only who collaborated. The extended art-historical disputes are compressed here; they supply a methodological warning, not a universal verdict on every sociological account. *Locator: chs. 2–5, especially ch. 3, “Bourdieu's Lesson,” and the transitions on causal models.*

### 6. The Baroque Case: the past as a practical reconstruction
The revival of Baroque performance involves surviving objects, treatises, embodied habits, new communities and contemporary trials of what sounds convincing. Historical instruments do not make a lost practice return by themselves. Their capacities become effective when people learn to use and hear them.

An appeal to origins can itself create new musical possibilities. Evaluate a revival by identifying the historical evidence and the present work of reconstruction. Do not choose between “faithful copy” and “modern fake” before examining the mediations. This gives a genealogy of attachment, not an automatic endorsement of any authenticity claim. *Locator: ch. 6, especially “Were the ‘Ancients’ More Innovative than the ‘Moderns’?”*

### 7. “What Can You Hear?”: becoming able to listen
The solfège lesson shows musical attention being achieved. Children, teacher, piano, bodily orientation, memory, notation and relations among classmates change together. The classroom does not begin with music fully present and pupils merely failing to receive it.

Use the case to investigate a listener's changing competence: what cue directs attention, what comparison becomes possible, and what material support lets the listener notice a difference? Do not reduce the change to obedience or to discovery of a preference that was there all along. Learning can reorganize the object that is heard. *Locator: ch. 7, “A Structural Change: From a Crowd to a Class.”*

### 8. “Bach Today”: making an enduring composer
The modern Bach is encountered through catalogues, editions, arrangements, recordings, scholarship and collecting. These are historical achievements, not packaging placed around an unchanged object. Earlier listeners worked with the Bach available to them; judging them against a later complete catalogue rewrites history backwards.

When building a record library, distinguish the work's production history from the later organization of its legacy. A complete edition, revival or reissue can create new relationships among works and listeners. Current availability is not evidence of how a repertory was originally known. *Locator: ch. 8.*

### 9. Music Lovers: taste as an activity
Treat the listener as an active starting point of musical experience, not the final receiver in a linear production chain. Ask how they prepare, compare, collect, repeat, discuss, attend and surrender attention. Respect practical competence without assuming every retrospective explanation is complete.

**Discomorphosis** names the transformation of music and its listeners around recorded repertories and record-oriented listening. Historical music becomes available as a collection of objects; this access can also obscure the practices that produced it. Intimate listening may feel uniquely personal while depending on widely shared formats and equipment.

**Taste as a reflexive performance** means that attachments and the capacities to experience them emerge through practical trials. Objects remain responsive: a passage, interpretation or sound can resist an expectation and change a listener. Social construction does not mean musical irrelevance.

The concert, too, is a trial in **making something happen**. Attention, reluctance, neighboring listeners, expectation and performance develop rather than arriving as a guaranteed effect. A disappointing encounter is evidence about an event, not necessarily a final verdict on a work or a person. *Locator: ch. 9, “A ‘Discomorphosis’ of Music,” “Making Something Happen,” “Taste as a Reflexive Performance.”*

### Conclusion: in praise of musical artifice
The opposition between a pure musical object and external mediation prevents an account of how music comes to matter. Examine the made arrangements through which an experience becomes possible. Artifice can be productive without being beyond criticism. The relevant question is what a mediation accomplishes, for whom, under which conditions. *Locator: Conclusion, “The Representation of Music.”*

## Worked Example
In the solfège lesson, an initially indifferent group becomes a class able to distinguish and reproduce musical relations. The teacher's movements, piano, stave, memory tasks and students' responses progressively reorganize attention. Hennion's account does not merely credit a charismatic teacher or a compelling tune; it shows their effects becoming possible through a series of mediations. For listener research, the analogous question is which concrete change in the listening arrangement enabled something previously unnoticed to matter. This analogy is a project application, not a tested training protocol. *Locator: ch. 7.*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When someone says “I just like it,” investigate how that liking becomes available without treating the report as ignorance.
- When taste changes across hearings, examine the trial: equipment, attention, company, familiarity and comparison, as well as the recording.
- When taste is reduced to class, ask what the object actually contributes; when reduced to acoustics, ask how the listener learned and arranged the encounter.
- When designing a comparison, allow the comparison itself to change attention; it is not a neutral measurement of an immutable preference.
- When an expert recommends a listening practice, offer it as an experiment with a possible failure, not compulsory cultural improvement.
- When preserving a reaction, retain the situation and the listener's own explanation alongside the analyst's tentative account.
- When organizing historical repertory, distinguish convenient access from original historical presence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Mediators participate in making musical objects and listeners.
2. Taste is a practical, reflexive activity.
3. Respect listener competence and the resistance of musical particulars.
4. Repetition and comparison can transform attachment.
5. Neither social reduction nor intrinsic-effect reduction is enough.
