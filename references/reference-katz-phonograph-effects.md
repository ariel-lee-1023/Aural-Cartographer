# Capturing Sound: How Technology Has Changed Music — Mark Katz

## Mental Model (read first)
Recording changes musical behavior rather than simply capturing something already complete. A **phonograph effect** is a change in listening, performance or composition arising in response to recording technology. The analytical task is to show how a property of a medium becomes consequential through people's use of it; technology is neither a sufficient cause nor a verdict on musical value.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1. Causes: the seven properties
Katz organizes the discussion around **tangibility, portability, (in)visibility, repeatability, temporality, receptivity, and manipulability**. These are prompts for tracing effects, not seven inevitable stages.

| Property | What it makes available or constrains | Evidence needed for an effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tangibility | Sound becomes a collectable, ownable material object | Changes in collecting, exchange or the conception of a work |
| Portability | Music travels without the original performers or setting | New encounters, circulation and uses beyond the original occasion |
| (In)visibility | Sound can be heard without seeing its source | Adaptation to an absent visual performance or audience |
| Repeatability | The same recorded details can be heard repeatedly | Learning, imitation, scrutiny, familiarity or attachment to a particular version |
| Temporality | Media organize duration and access through time | Constraints on length, selection, sequencing or continuity |
| Receptivity | A recording system captures some sounds more effectively than others | Adjustments of instrumentation, placement, balance or execution |
| Manipulability | Recorded sound can be altered, assembled or repurposed | New musical relationships that depend on such operations |

Keep an affordance separate from a realized practice. Repeatability can support careful study or rote imitation; the property does not decide between them. A contemporary application requires evidence about the actual medium, not merely a historical analogy. *Locator: ch. 1.*

### 2. Making America More Musical: the phonograph and “good music”
Advocates presented the phonograph as a way to spread valued music into homes and education. It widened access while also advancing particular hierarchies of what counted as worthwhile listening. Playing music and playing a recording did not require the same skills or distribute authority in the same way.

Ask whose repertory and listening ideal an access project promotes. Greater availability is a condition for some encounters, not evidence that everyone listens more deeply or benefits identically. Do not tell a simple story in which the device either democratizes or destroys musical life. *Locator: ch. 2.*

### 3. Capturing Jazz
Early jazz records participate in the music's development but do not transparently represent the whole practice. Time limits, capture conditions, absent visual cues and repeated circulation change what is performed and learned. The first widely recognized jazz recording is not the origin of jazz, and its performers do not represent everyone making it.

When using a recording as historical evidence, identify the format's constraints and the selection that brought it into the studio. A solo captured for repeated listening can become a model in ways a fleeting performance could not. Distinguish a recording's later pedagogical significance from the status it had at the session. *Locator: ch. 3.*

### 4. Aesthetics out of Exigency: violin vibrato
Katz proposes recording as an important contributor to the spread of conspicuous, relatively continuous violin vibrato. The practical demands of recorded sound may help shape an aesthetic subsequently heard as normal.

This is an argued historical explanation, not proof of a single cause. Look for the proposed relationship between capture or playback conditions, performer adaptation and changing standards. Do not infer that a technique arising partly from practical constraints is artistically inferior, or that every later use repeats the original cause. *Locator: ch. 4.*

### 5. The Rise and Fall of Grammophonmusik
**Grammophonmusik** explores the record as a compositional medium rather than a reproductive afterthought. Works made specifically through or for records complicate a history that assigns all such invention to later technologies.

Identify what the work could not do without its medium, and distinguish technical possibility from a sustained practice with institutions and audiences. Historical disappearance may reflect preservation and reception, not a lack of invention. *Locator: ch. 5.*

### 6. The Turntable as Weapon: the hip-hop DJ battle
Turntablists turn playback equipment into instruments. The practice combines technique, timing, quotation, bodily display, competition and audience knowledge. A description of the device alone cannot explain a battle's meaning or skill criteria.

Analyze the transformation of recorded material and the conventions by which participants hear a boast, joke or challenge. Do not reduce the performance either to pressing play or to a disembodied sound collage. The record is a material resource in a live social encounter. *Locator: ch. 6.*

### 7. Music in 1s and 0s: digital sampling
**Digital sampling** incorporates a portion of an existing recording into another. What travels is a particular recorded sound, not only an abstract melody or compositional idea. Selection, repetition, processing and context can change its role while preserving traces of an earlier performance.

Keep several questions distinct: what sound is reused, how the new work organizes it, what relationship listeners recognize, and which people receive credit or control. Musical transformation does not settle current legal permission. The book's historical legal discussion is not current legal advice. *Locator: ch. 7.*

### 8. Listening in Cyberspace
Digital circulation changes search, collecting, availability and the relation between recordings and information about them. The dated services in the revised edition are cases, not current recommendations. Use them to identify changes in access and practice.

Ease of obtaining a file is not equivalent to understanding its music or preserving provenance. Ask how a discovery system organizes attention, which version it supplies, and what context is lost or newly available. *Locator: ch. 8.*

### Conclusion: users determine impact and value
Katz explicitly rejects a technologically deterministic reading. The same property supports divergent activities; users also reshape technologies. For Aural-Cartographer, a useful explanatory chain is medium property → situated adaptation → musical consequence → evidence and alternatives. This is a project formulation of the book's approach, not a new causal law. *Locator: Conclusion, “Users Determine the Impact and Value of Recording.”*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When saying technology changed music, identify the property, the adaptation and evidence linking them.
- When using early records historically, account for what could be captured and what was selected.
- When a performance technique appears “natural,” investigate the recording practices that may have normalized it.
- When assessing sampling, distinguish transformation, genealogy, attribution and permission.
- When evaluating fidelity, specify fidelity to what: a live occasion, a work, an imagined sound or a particular recording.
- When recommending across genres, a shared use of a medium can justify an analogy; it does not prove influence.
- When describing a historical device's effects, retain human choices and divergent outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Recording is a participant in musical history.
2. A phonograph effect needs a demonstrated change in practice.
3. Media properties enable and constrain without dictating value.
4. Reuse can preserve a sound while transforming its meaning.
5. Recordings require interpretation as historical evidence.
