## Mental Model (read first)
Expectation prepares an organism for possible futures; musical experiences recruit those processes in learned and culturally situated ways. Huron separates several responses that everyday descriptions collapse into “tension” or “surprise.” His proposals explain possible mechanisms of feeling, not a universal standard of musical value or an infallible model of one listener.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1–2. ITPRA and the paradox of pleasurable surprise
**ITPRA** stands for **Imagination, Tension, Prediction, Reaction, Appraisal**. It distinguishes responses before and after an outcome; it is not a rigid five-step conscious sequence.

| Response | Relevant process | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| Imagination | Vicarious feeling from imagining an outcome | What possible future is being entertained? |
| Tension | Preparatory arousal and attention as an outcome approaches | What is expected, when, and with what uncertainty? |
| Prediction | Valence associated with predictive success or failure | Was the expectation fulfilled, independently of whether the outcome was desirable? |
| Reaction | Rapid, relatively automatic assessment and bodily response | What immediate response may precede reflection? |
| Appraisal | Slower, context-sensitive assessment, able to revise or reinforce a reaction | How is the event understood once its implications are considered? |

An expected bad event can combine successful prediction with an unpleasant outcome. An unexpected good event can combine predictive failure with positive appraisal. **Contrastive valence** proposes that the contrast between an initially negative surprise response and a benign or positive appraisal can intensify pleasure. It is a conditional account, not a claim that every surprise is enjoyable.

Huron discusses laughter, awe and frisson as distinguishable outcomes. Do not infer one from the mere presence of a loud entrance or unexpected harmony. *Locator: chs. 1–2.*

### 3. Measuring expectation
Methods include tone detection, production, probe-tone judgments, betting, head-turning, bradycardic response, reaction time and event-related potentials. They measure different responses under different tasks; none is simply a direct readout of aesthetic taste.

Distinguish **subjective probability**, the listener's expectation of an event, from its frequency in a corpus. **Uncertainty** concerns the distribution over possible outcomes; it is not identical to the improbability of the event that eventually occurs. For an informal listening comparison, a report of what was expected and when is useful, but it is not a calibrated probability or physiological measure. *Locator: ch. 3.*

### 4–6. Learning and melodic regularities
**Statistical learning** concerns acquiring patterns and conditional relations through exposure. An event's probability depends on context; counting notes without their preceding sequence can miss the expectation that matters. Learning may occur without explicit verbal knowledge.

Huron examines **pitch proximity** (nearby continuations), **step declination** (the prominence of small descending motion), **step inertia** (continuing a stepwise direction), **melodic regression** (movement back toward a central pitch region), and **melodic arch** (a tendency to rise and then fall across a span). These describe different tendencies and hypotheses; their prevalence in a corpus, their representation by listeners and their causal origins require separate evidence. In particular, a tendency for leaps to be followed by a reversal must be distinguished from a listener's rule and from regression associated with a bounded pitch range.

**Heuristic listening** explains why a useful learned shortcut may diverge from the actual statistics. The book discusses Narmour and Schenker critically rather than simply equating their theories with mental processing. Avoid deriving an individual listener's expectations directly from a notated structure. *Locator: chs. 4–6.*

### 7. Mental representation of expectation I
The same sound can be represented through different relational features. Pitch height, pitch class, interval, scale degree and other representations are not interchangeable; experience affects which becomes useful for prediction. Do not assume that identifying an interval in analysis identifies what the listener's prediction system used.

This chapter's broader account of acquiring useful auditory representations is retained as a qualification on mechanism claims. Its neural proposals are not treated here as current neuroscientific consensus. *Locator: ch. 7.*

### 8–9. Prediction effect, familiarity and tonality
The **prediction effect** concerns feelings from accurate prediction being attributed to the predicted stimulus. It offers an account of some pleasures of familiarity and repetition. The **exposure effect** does not imply that unlimited repetition always improves enjoyment.

Tonal **qualia**—such as stability, closure or tendency—are examined in relation to learned distributions and contexts. Huron distinguishes mechanisms from aesthetic goals and explicitly notes differences among listeners and listening aims. A familiar cadence may be satisfying, banal, reassuring or inappropriate to a particular project; expectation theory alone cannot choose among those judgments. *Locator: chs. 8–9, especially “Aesthetic Caveats” and “Tonality and Culture.”*

### 10. Expectation in time
Separate expectations about **what** occurs from expectations about **when** it occurs. Periodicity, tactus, metrical hierarchy and longer contextual relations help organize temporal preparation. A predicted downbeat can accrue positive feeling through predictive success; syncopated events can resist a maintained metric expectation.

Timing explanations require identifying the listener's operative beat or grouping, not merely the score's bar lines. Speech-like and nonperiodic timing also generate expectations. A regular pulse is one route, not the definition of musical expectation. *Locator: ch. 10.*

### 11–12. Schemas, memory and concurrent expectations
**Schematic expectations** derive from generalized learned patterns, often associated with a style. **Veridical expectations** concern a particular known sequence or work. **Dynamic expectations** develop during the unfolding piece through short-term exposure. **Conscious expectations** arise from explicit knowledge, instruction or reflection.

These can coexist and conflict. Knowing precisely when a familiar event will happen need not eliminate a response grounded in broader learned norms. **Context cueing** selects a schema through learned environmental markers; errors may trigger **schema switching**. **Firewalls** describe the separation of learned expectational domains rather than a single undifferentiated statistical model.

For discovery, ask which familiarity is missing. An unfamiliar style, an unknown track in a familiar style and a new pattern within a track pose different problems. Do not label all three “novelty.” *Locator: chs. 11–12.*

### 13. Creating predictability
Predictability can arise from knowledge of a particular work, stylistic schemata or patterns established locally through repetition, sequence and thematic recurrence. Specify which process supports an expected continuation. Mere repetition does not explain every pleasure of a repeated phrase; timbre, social association and performance can remain decisive. *Locator: ch. 13.*

### 14. Creating surprise
Distinguish schematic, veridical and dynamic violations. A **garden path surprise** requires revising an earlier interpretation in light of a later event. That differs from an isolated odd event within a stable interpretation.

Repeated listening can change kinds of response differently. Huron contrasts the decline of musical humor with the possible persistence of frisson. Use this as a reason to separate reported responses, not as a guarantee that chills will persist for every listener. *Locator: ch. 14, especially “Garden Path Surprises” and “Effects of Repeated Listening.”*

### 15. Creating tension
A delayed expected outcome can prolong preparation and intensify tension. Suspensions, anticipations, uncertain continuations, premonitions and climaxes offer different temporal configurations. Before invoking this account, identify an expected outcome and the evidence that it was expected.

An increase in volume or density may contribute without proving a prediction mechanism. Keep a sonic description, a listener's report and the proposed mechanism separate. *Locator: ch. 15, “Emotional Effect of Delay.”*

### 16–17. Expecting the unexpected; aesthetic boundaries
Contracadential, contratonal and contrametric practices can systematically resist learned expectations. Their analysis requires the relevant learning history; resistance to a familiar grammar is not an absence of organization.

The conclusion returns to the distinction between psychological mechanisms and musical purposes. Cross-cultural access to a sound does not establish understanding of its ritual, social, linguistic or musical significance. Contemporary empirical status and population generality need research beyond this 2006 book. *Locator: chs. 16–17.*

## Worked Example
In the delay account, a listener anticipates an outcome at a particular moment. Preparatory tension builds toward that moment; postponement can sustain it until the outcome arrives. The explanation changes if the listener did not expect that event, did not hear the timing the same way, or was attending elsewhere. In the book's discussion of appoggiatura and suspension, the value of the analysis is this temporal differentiation, not a universal equation between dissonance and emotion. A proposed listening test should compare first and familiar hearings while recording the expected event, reported tension and alternative explanations. The test is a project adaptation. *Locator: ch. 15, including the appoggiatura discussion near printed p. 315.*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When “surprise” explains everything, separate prediction, immediate reaction and later appraisal.
- When familiarity and surprise coexist, distinguish veridical from schematic expectations.
- When a pattern is called universal, identify the population and learning history actually supported.
- When proposing a mechanism for a listener, ask what observation could distinguish it from timbre, memory or context.
- When pleasure is observed, do not infer aesthetic superiority.
- When a detail persists in its effect across repetitions, do not assume all surprise has disappeared or that one mechanism has been proven.
- When a source contains damaged notation, do not invent its pitches, probabilities or experimental results.

## Key Takeaways
1. ITPRA separates processes that can yield mixed emotions.
2. Expectation depends on representation, context and learning.
3. Knowing a track and knowing a style are different.
4. Musical mechanisms do not prescribe aesthetic goals.
5. Listener reports support hypotheses, not automatic causal certainty.
