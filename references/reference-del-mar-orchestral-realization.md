## Mental Model (read first)

A score's instrumental assignments become sound through instruments with unequal capacities, players making practical judgments and an ensemble balancing relationships. Register, technique, numerical strength and acoustic circumstances affect which functions emerge, fuse or disappear. The same harmonic inventory can therefore have different expressive and formal force under different orchestral realizations.

Del Mar writes as a conductor examining the practical life of scores, including ambiguities and problematic conventions. His preferences and historical claims remain attributed, especially where practice varies across periods and orchestras. The analytical procedures connecting his details to formal function and recorded presentation below are Aural Cartographer's synthesis.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Section I: Strings — distribution, technique and mass

**Section versus solo:** a solo line and a section in unison are different sonic resources even at the same pitch and dynamic. Identify the number and distribution of players actually supported by the score or documentation; a dense-looking page does not establish a large sounding body.

**Divided playing (divisi):** dividing at a desk and dividing by desks distribute players differently. More separate lines need not mean more players or greater weight per line. Ask whether a division creates independent functions, a blended field or a thinner articulation of the same material. Detailed desk allocation is needed only where it explains the result. *Locator: pp. 36–49, “Divided Playing.”*

**Numerical strength:** Del Mar describes varied string forces and cases where the composer limits them for balance. The operative relationship is to the other parts and the setting; there is no timeless ideal section size. Do not adopt his early-1980s account of typical personnel as a current universal standard. *Locator: pp. 62–65.*

**Platform planning:** opposite violin groups can make exchanges more distinct, while separation can introduce ensemble difficulties in a wide or unhelpful acoustic. Del Mar favors some antiphonal effects but acknowledges rehearsal and practical limits. Seating can change the realization of a written relation without changing the notes. Recorded stereo position is a separate evidential question. *Locator: pp. 50–61, especially p. 54.*

**Register and string choice:** an instrument's high or low register is not a fixed emotional label. A line's position within the instrument's compass, its exposure and its neighbors change weight, tension and projection. Written pitch also needs correct clef and sounding-octave interpretation. The detailed compass and harmonics tables are omitted rather than reconstructed from OCR. *Locators: pp. 105–122.*

**Bowing, plucking and resonance:** the manner of initiating and sustaining a note shapes the line's rhythmic and textural function. Pizzicato, sustained bowing and tremolo are not interchangeable colors on otherwise identical sound. Use a verified technique marking or suitable audio evidence before describing a particular realization; the full inventory of bow strokes is outside this reference. *Locators: section I, bowing and non-bowed-style chapters.*

**Muting:** con sordini changes timbre as well as level. It can remain significant in a loud passage. Missing or disputed instructions to remove mutes create an interpretive question, not permission to infer a standard unmuted climax. *Locator: pp. 123–125.*

### Section II: Woodwind — family identity is conditional on register

**Family characteristics:** the practical question is what a particular line can project and how its sound combines with its neighbors. Avoid a list of fixed personalities such as “flute = light” or “bassoon = comic.” Del Mar's examples repeatedly cross those stereotypes.

**Flutes and piccolo:** the flute's lower register offers timbral possibilities quite different from its upper one; a low piccolo can be delicate and poorly penetrating despite the instrument's high sounding register. Instrument name and written height therefore cannot substitute for a register-sensitive balance judgment. Del Mar also describes differing schools of flute tone. *Locator: pp. 165–168.*

**Clarinet registers:** the chalumeau region has characteristic color and soft control; upper writing presents a different balance and intonation problem. The register break makes an apparently simple contour a practical issue. The original's precise pitch examples must be checked in legible notation before reuse. *Locator: pp. 173–175.*

**Bassoon beyond the bass:** sustained upper lines can have a markedly different character from low passages or detached figurations. The bassoon can also reinforce a horn texture. Function and combination matter more than treating a family as confined to its nominal orchestral rank. *Locator: pp. 176–178.*

**Blend is negotiated:** woodwind chords require adjustment of pitch, attack, tone and sometimes vibrato among unlike instruments. Del Mar disputes the idea that arranging timbres by a tidy abstract formula guarantees the result. Experienced players can make momentary adjustments that a score does not explicitly encode. An analytically simple chord may be difficult to balance or tune. *Locator: pp. 210–213.*

**Attack and audibility:** a written simultaneous onset is not a complete description of the achieved ensemble attack. Exposure, readiness of the instrument, breath and individual production enter the result. Describe audible alignment or testimony without diagnosing an unheard player's mistake. *Locator: pp. 211–213.*

### Section III: Horns — connecting and differentiating families

**Orchestral function:** Del Mar places horns between woodwind and heavy brass in practical responsibility. Their ability to blend with woodwind can be as consequential as their more forceful ensemble role. Classifying the material “brass” does not settle how it functions in a passage. *Locator: p. 215.*

**Reinforcement across families:** horns and bassoons can form a closely matched texture, and bassoons can supplement horn writing. A doubling can make a single function rounder or heavier without adding an independent melodic event. Conversely, contrasting register or articulation may make the contributors separable. The first claim is grounded in Del Mar's examples; the latter is the critical question to test in a new passage. *Locator: pp. 177–178, with section III.*

**Notation and instruments:** transposition, historical crooks and later instruments complicate the route from written appearance to sounding pitch and character. State whether an account uses written or sounding pitch. Do not silently read a historical horn part as though all notation were standardized. Del Mar's preference for transposed scores is his practical judgment, not a universal instruction for analysis. *Locators: pp. 216–222, 236–243.*

**Stopped and muted effects:** these are separately treated in the source and must not be collapsed into a generic softer horn. Identify the specific instruction and its historical context if it is relevant; this reference does not supply fingering, exact transposition corrections or a technique tutorial. *Locators: “Open and Stopped Notes,” pp. 236–239; “Muting,” pp. 243–249.*

### Section IV: Heavy brass — articulation and dynamic contrast

**Trumpets, trombones and tubas:** a family's participation is not automatic in every full orchestral score. Check the particular forces and their deployment instead of inferring a standard maximal complement from the label “orchestra.” *Locator: pp. 273–274.*

**Incisiveness and weight:** brass can articulate rhythm with great force, while hall and studio conditions can complicate perceived alignment. A dominant attack can affect the ensemble's sense of motion. Del Mar also emphasizes very soft playing and sudden hushed contrast; brass is not synonymous with loud climax. *Locator: pp. 323–324.*

**Register and exposure:** a prominent upper trumpet entry demands a different practical and expressive account from a blended inner voice. Range, endurance and exposed placement can affect realization, but they do not justify inferring strain or failure from notation alone. *Locator: pp. 324–326.*

**Balance as relation:** adding brass may reinforce an existing harmonic function, separate an arrival from its preparation or obscure an inner line. Compare the functions before and after the entrance. These are analytical possibilities to test, not claims that any brass addition must have one of those effects. Mutes, dynamics and placement need passage-specific evidence.

### Section V: Timpani and percussion — shaping attacks and their aftermath

**Beater choice:** Del Mar contrasts the clarity available from harder timpani sticks with the resonance of softer ones. Some choices are left to performers even where the score gives no instruction. A change in rhythmic definition may thus be a realization decision rather than new composition. No exact stick type can be established from a vague description. *Locator: pp. 359–360.*

**Muting and damping:** suppressing or terminating resonance changes how an event occupies time. Distinguish the onset from its continuing sound and from a notated value. What sounds through a rest can alter texture and the next attack's clarity. *Locators: pp. 366–368 and the individual percussion discussions.*

**Accumulated resonance:** a tam-tam can continue sounding long after a brief stroke; repeated strokes can build enough resonance to blur rhythmic articulation. Greater mass can therefore reduce the distinctness of the pattern. Del Mar's caution connects density and temporal consequence, not simply loudness. *Locator: pp. 400–401.*

An orchestral punctuation can establish a boundary, connect across it through resonance or cover a simultaneous detail. Determine which occurs from the actual score/performance relation. The inventory of unusual percussion and its historical nomenclature is omitted because it does not by itself improve this critical decision.

### Section VI: Keyboard and other instruments — sustain and integration

**Harp resonance:** the harp has no sustaining pedal or built-in damping mechanism comparable to a piano's. Players use hands and arms to control decay, frequently without detailed score directions. Deliberately extending or cutting resonance is an orchestral technique. *Locator: pp. 448–449.*

**Terminology depends on instrument and context:** Del Mar explains an instance of “con sord.” in a harp part as immediate damping, not the installation of an ordinary mute. A shared word across parts does not guarantee a shared physical action or sound. *Locator: p. 449.*

**Keyboard support:** attack, resonance and registral reinforcement can matter more than whether the listener recognizes a separate keyboard line. The relevant question is which function is made more distinct, sustained or fused. The detailed piano, celesta, organ and miscellaneous-instrument surveys remain outside this selective reference; consult a reliable passage-specific source for their exact techniques and limits.

## Worked Example

**The muted climax in Dvořák's New World Symphony, Largo** (section I, p. 123, visually checked).

Del Mar notes that the central climactic string passage is often played without mutes although the score contains no instruction to remove them. He argues for the distinctive effect of retaining the muted sound, including at a strong dynamic.

The critical problem has three stages: determine the edition's instruction; establish what the performers actually do; explain how that choice changes the character of the climax. “Louder means unmuted” fails at the first stage because dynamic level and timbral technique are separate. Del Mar's preferred effect is an attributed judgment, not proof of the composer's private intention. A recording comparison would additionally have to consider how microphone placement and mixing present the result. This example does not identify what happens on any unexamined recording.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Replace “richer orchestration” with the consequential change: register, family, doubling, number of independent functions, density, blend, contrast or balance.
- Before describing redistribution, verify sounding pitch and which parts actually play; credits and a secondary adjective cannot establish either.
- Separate independent lines from doublings and section division. More staves, more players and more perceptual density are different claims.
- Ask whether a new timbre exposes, reinforces, fuses with or masks an existing function, then connect that change to phrasing, arrival or expressive contrast.
- Treat mutes, articulation and damping as changes in temporal/timbral behavior, not only volume controls.
- If equal notes produce unequal results, investigate player balance, attack, intonation, seating and acoustic conditions before concluding the composition changed.
- Distinguish written orchestration, performed realization and recorded presentation. A mix can expose an inner part without proving that the orchestral balance in the hall was identical.
- Pair Rosen for historically situated sonority and formal/expressive judgment; Moore/Zak for layering and the recorded object; structural sources for the harmonic or formal event being realized. These bridges do not turn Del Mar into a production manual.

## Key Takeaways

1. Instrumental capacity and register change what a written relationship can sound like.
2. Doubling, division and independent parts have different effects on weight and clarity.
3. Blend and balance emerge through performance as well as notation.
4. Attacks and resonance shape temporal organization.
5. Orchestral transformation can constitute a formal event even with stable harmony.
6. Source-era practice and a conductor's preferences are not universal prescriptions.
