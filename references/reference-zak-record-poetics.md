## Mental Model (read first)
The rock record is a made musical work whose identity includes its particular sounds. Recording is compositional activity: performances, devices, decisions and accidents become a fixed configuration that listeners encounter repeatedly. Analyze the result as an aesthetic construction without pretending that listening reveals every step of its production history.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1. Writing Records: the recorded work
Zak brings the **autographic musical work** argument, associated here with Theodore Gracyk and Nelson Goodman's distinctions, into the practical world of record making. What matters for this inquiry is the specificity of the recorded sonic inscription. A lead sheet or a new performance does not preserve everything that makes a particular record that work.

The method follows **poetics**: how works are made, with attention to practitioners' decisions and materials. Studio tools belong inside musical explanation when they shape the result. This does not require treating technical novelty as aesthetic success or denying the independent life of a song. *Locator: ch. 1.*

### 2. Tracks: three compositional layers
**Song** comprises what can ordinarily be represented by a lead sheet: words, melody, harmony and some formal design. **Arrangement** is a particular setting: instrumentation, parts, rhythmic groove and their organization. **Track** is the recording itself; in hearing it, the listener encounters song and arrangement through the actual recorded sounds.

The layers can develop in different orders. A song may be finished before recording, or writing and arranging may continue in the studio. Do not infer chronology from the conceptual distinction. For criticism, ask where the consequential choice resides: a melodic idea, a distribution of parts, a performance gesture, a spatial image, or their interaction. For versions, distinguish a reinterpretation of a song from a change to the mix or presentation of a recording. *Locator: ch. 2, opening discussion, printed pp. 24–25.*

### 3. Sound as Form: five domains of recorded sound
Zak groups recorded phenomena into **musical performance, timbre, echo, ambience (reverberation), and texture**. These domains interact; the same device can affect several. Follow the configuration through time rather than making five independent checklists.

**Musical performance:** timing, articulation, energy, phrasing and interaction become compositional material. A compelling performance need not be technically immaculate. Conversely, looseness does not prove vitality. Explain which gesture matters, in relation to what, and where.

**Timbre:** tone colour establishes character, contrast and continuity. The relevant object is the sound heard on the record, not only an instrument's nominal acoustic identity. Changes of colour can mark form and dramatic development. A distortion-like effect does not, without documentation, identify the device or settings that produced it.

**Echo:** distinguish a perceptible delayed replication from a diffuse ambience. Repetitions can supply rhythmic activity, alter apparent density or create a response to a source. Ask what an echo does to the phrase and its space, rather than describing it merely as an effect added afterwards.

**Ambience (reverberation):** the surrounding acoustic image can suggest a place and establish relations of distance, scale and continuity. A constructed space need not imitate a plausible room. “Large,” “distant” and “reverberant” do not identify a specific hall, chamber or processor.

**Texture:** relationships among sources, registers and sound images create an evolving musical surface. Density, prominence and spatial contrast can shape form even when chords repeat. Ask how the texture supports or complicates a vocal or instrumental event. *Locator: ch. 3, especially the opening comparison and subsections on echo and ambience.*

### 4. Places and Tools: an audible result is made through a chain
The recordist works through source sound, room acoustics, the signal path, recording medium and monitoring conditions. Decisions are empirical and depend on how the sound is heard in that setting. An apparently nonmusical choice can have musical consequences.

For a documented production history, trace choice → change in sound image → compositional consequence. For a listening-only account, stop at the audible consequence and mark any proposed cause as a hypothesis. Different tools can yield similar results, and nominally identical equipment can serve different aesthetics. Avoid equating analog, vintage, expensive or technically clean with good. *Locator: ch. 4.*

### 5. Tracking and Mixing: progressive specification
Tracking generates material and develops the work; mixing brings its final relationships into focus. The stages are interdependent, even when separated by time, place or personnel. Comping and editing may complete a performance; later changes in timbre or ambience can alter its meaning.

A finished track should therefore not be assumed to document a continuous event. Nor should construction be used as evidence that nothing was performed. Distinguish what musicians did, how performances were selected or combined, and how the recording stages them.

Zak describes a practical balance between control and openness to unforeseen events. Production can keep a project coherent while preserving accidents and provisional discoveries. The critic should not reconstruct an elegant master plan from every effective result. *Locator: ch. 5.*

### 6. Engineers and Producers: the composition team
The credited artist is not necessarily the sole maker of a record's aesthetic identity. Engineers and producers contribute perception, technical choices, social coordination and judgment. These roles vary across projects; a title in the credits alone does not prove authorship of a particular sound.

For attribution, pair a verified credit with a documented contribution. Distinguish session testimony, retrospective recollection and inference from the finished record. Trust and compatibility can alter what performers attempt and what is captured. Social collaboration explains part of the result without replacing close analysis of that result. *Locator: ch. 6.*

### 7. Resonance: records heard through other records
**Resonance** describes the accumulated field of conventions, associations, allusions and remembered records through which new work becomes meaningful. A timbre or spatial arrangement can evoke another recording without quoting its melody.

For genealogy, distinguish a listener's useful association from an attested production influence. For discovery, a shared recording strategy can be a route across genres: compare how two tracks build distance or instrumental weight. This is an analytical analogy unless evidence establishes historical transmission. A critic's association is not the listener's inevitable association. *Locator: ch. 7.*

## Worked Example
Zak compares Bob Dylan's and Jimi Hendrix's recordings of “All Along the Watchtower.” The song remains a common basis, but performance urgency, timbral range, echo, ambience and texture transform its dramatic world. Zak reads Dylan's relative austerity differently from the force and spatial activity of Hendrix's construction. The comparison demonstrates why a song-level account cannot explain both tracks adequately. Use the example as Zak's interpretation, not proof that those sonic features must always mean resignation or defiance. *Locator: ch. 3, opening, printed pp. 48 onward.*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When a review discusses only writing and lyrics, ask what the track contributes that another realization could change.
- When “overproduced” or “raw” appears, identify the concrete relation being praised or criticized and its consequence for the work.
- When echo and reverb are conflated, determine whether discrete repetitions or an ambient field is doing the relevant work; remain tentative if evidence cannot distinguish them.
- When the record feels spontaneous, do not infer a single live take; when it feels polished, do not infer emotional emptiness.
- When attributing a production decision, require documentation beyond a credit or stylistic resemblance.
- When a happy accident matters, preserve its contribution without inventing a planned intention.
- When comparing versions, hold song identity separate from arrangement and track identity.
- When recommending by “sound,” specify the transferable sonic relationship rather than matching a production adjective.

## Key Takeaways
1. The record itself is the primary object for recorded-music criticism.
2. Song, arrangement and track make different contributions.
3. Spatial and timbral relationships can carry form and meaning.
4. Production is collaborative and contingent.
5. Heard effects and historical causes require different evidence.
