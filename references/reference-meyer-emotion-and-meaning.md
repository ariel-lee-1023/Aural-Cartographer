## Mental Model (read first)

Music can mean through the tendencies one event establishes toward possible subsequent events. Affect can arise when an activated tendency is delayed, inhibited or redirected, provided the listener has learned enough of the style to form it. Meaning develops through time and retrospective understanding. Musical structure, affective response, connotation and aesthetic judgment therefore have relations without being identical.

## Frameworks & Structure

### I. Theory: Do not confuse two different distinctions

- **Absolutist / referentialist:** whether meaning is understood within musical relations or through reference beyond them. The two kinds can coexist. Meyer's concentration on intramusical meaning does not deny extramusical association.
- **Formalist / expressionist:** whether those musical relationships are treated principally as objects of intellectual understanding or as capable of arousing affect. An expressionist can be an absolutist; musical feeling need not depend on a story or represented emotion. These axes must not be collapsed into “technical” versus “emotional.”
- **Affect and tendency:** a tendency is an activated orientation toward an outcome, not merely a consciously predicted next note. Inhibition or conflict can generate affect. Distinguish general arousal from a named emotion and from a listener's later verbal account of it.
- **Specific / nonspecific expectation:** a passage may strongly suggest one continuation or make change/completion likely without specifying exactly which event will provide it. Uncertainty is not identical to the surprise of one realized event.
- **Embodied meaning:** a musical stimulus acquires meaning through the possible and actual musical events it indicates in a style and context. This differs from **designative meaning**, in which a sign refers beyond the musical succession. “Embodied” here is Meyer's term for intramusical meaning, not a synonym for contemporary embodied-cognition theory.
- **Hypothetical meaning:** possibilities envisaged before a consequent arrives. **Evident meaning:** the relation understood once that consequent occurs. **Determinate meaning:** retrospective relations grasped when the whole is available in memory. These can occur at multiple architectonic levels; a later event can revise the meaning of an earlier one. Source: chapter I, especially pp. 35–39.
- **A strong theoretical limit:** Meyer treats wholly unfamiliar style as lacking embodied musical meaning in his defined sense because stylistic expectation is absent. Preserve the scope and contestability of that claim: it does not establish an absence of sensation, association, curiosity or value in an unfamiliar encounter.
- **Meaningful experience versus objectified meaning:** someone can respond intelligently to musical relations without naming them. An analyst's articulate explanation is not proof that the listener consciously performed that analysis.

### II. Expectation and learning

**Style** supplies learned probability relationships among musical events. It is not merely a visual label on a period or genre. An expected outcome depends on the context and the listener's acquired dispositions. A rule from a tonal harmony textbook is evidence about a style system, not a timeless psychological law.

Different degrees of stylistic competence can change what counts as orderly, surprising or ambiguous. A familiar work also permits knowledge of its particular events; do not confuse familiarity with the work and competence in its broader idiom. Learning can modify the expectation rather than simply suppress the feeling.

**Belief** matters: a listener who trusts a work to make its continuation intelligible may tolerate uncertainty differently from one who hears the same passage as random or incompetent. Such trust cannot be presumed for every listener, and a theory of that trust does not oblige an aesthetic endorsement. Source: chapter II, “Probability,” pp. 54–55, and the discussion of beliefs and attitudes.

### III. Pattern perception: The law of good continuation

**Good continuation** concerns tendencies to continue an established pattern or directed process. Identify the particular pattern, the parameter in which it is established and what would count as continuation. A broad appeal to “Gestalt” without those details explains little.

Grouping draws on similarity, proximity, relative emphasis and the relation of figure to ground. Rhythm and meter interact without being the same: a metric reference can persist while the grouping of durations and accents changes. Meter printed in a score is not sufficient evidence that a listener organizes the passage that way.

A purported polymetric structure must make its component organizations perceptible. Meyer's Debussy discussion notes that a relatively uniform part may function as ground instead of registering as an independently articulated meter. Thus the analyst's ability to count conflicting patterns does not guarantee their simultaneous perceptual salience. Source: chapter III, especially pp. 121–123.

### IV. Pattern completion and closure

**Completion and closure:** an incomplete or interrupted configuration can direct attention toward a more stable or complete one. Identify the interruption and the anticipated completion at the relevant level; a local close can remain open within a larger process.

**Gap filling:** a discontinuity can invite continuation within a contextually established field. Specify the register, scale or temporal pattern that makes the gap consequential. A leap alone is not a universal instruction to reverse direction; compare Huron's statistical and heuristic qualifications.

**Saturation:** persistent repetition can create an expectation of change when continuation or completion seems blocked. The condition matters: repetition at an intelligible ending, or repetition whose purpose is otherwise understood, need not produce saturation. Do not carry an expectation of developmental change unquestioned into a groove, drone, ritual or minimalist process. Source: chapter IV, “Saturation,” Example 44.

### V. The weakening of shape

Both excessive segregation and undifferentiated uniformity can weaken recognizable shape in Meyer's account. Perceptual articulation depends on the relative level of similarity, proximity and contrast in the style and immediate context. A small change can be decisive in one texture and negligible in another.

Weakening a shape can create uncertainty and a tendency toward clarification. Trace the actual changes—fragmentation, loss of clear grouping, overlap, ambiguity—before predicting this effect. Treat the claim as a model of a particular kind of musical apprehension; it does not establish that textural diffusion or deliberate stasis is an artistic failure. Source: chapter V, especially pp. 157–159.

### VI. Performance and tonal organization

The score or oral model does not mechanically fix its realization. Timing, accent, pitch inflection, ornament and phrasing can create or alter deviations and their effects. Meyer describes the performer as an active creator, with historically varying freedom. Source: chapter VI, pp. 199–205.

The boundary between a compositional event, an ornament and an expressive deviation can move across styles and periods. A suspension written into a score and a performer's agogic delay can both affect expectation, but they are different evidence and different actions. Do not infer actual rubato from a harmonic analysis or infer intention from a timing difference alone.

Tonal organization supplies graded relations of stability and tendency within a learned system. Name the local reference system before interpreting chromaticism or deviation. Pitch spacing and harmonic function can contribute different kinds of instability; neither can be read directly as the listener's emotion.

### VII. Simultaneous and successive deviation

A deviation may concern concurrent relationships or the order and timing of successive events. Several parameters can reinforce or counteract one another. Harmonic delay, regular pulse and increasing density can produce a different situation from harmonic delay accompanied by thinning texture and slowing tempo.

Work at more than one architectonic level: a local resolution can fulfill one tendency while initiating another; a fulfilled expectation can become the antecedent for a new one. Avoid a single scalar “tension score” that hides these simultaneous relations.

### VIII. Image processes, connotations and moods

**Connotations** can develop through cultural association and repeated contiguity. Instrumental, stylistic and melodic signs can reinforce one another, but their associations are not natural or uniform across cultures. An instrument considered exotic by one audience need not carry that association for another.

A represented mood, an evoked image and an experienced emotion are not equivalent. A listener may recognize a mournful convention without feeling grief. Meyer's historical examples and terminology reflect his time; retain the lesson about cultural learning while checking specific cross-cultural claims against appropriate evidence. Source: chapter VIII, especially pp. 258–261.

### Cross-round comparison: Meyer and Huron

| Question | Meyer | Huron |
|---|---|---|
| Main explanatory focus | Meaning and affect through stylistic tendencies, inhibition and unfolding relations | Distinct anticipatory and outcome responses in ITPRA |
| Intellectual/evidential setting | Gestalt organization, philosophical distinctions, musical examples and mid-century psychology | Cognitive and evolutionary proposals, statistical learning, experimental methods and musical corpora |
| Time | Hypothetical, evident and determinate meaning; multiple architectonic levels | Imagination/tension before an outcome; prediction, reaction and appraisal distinguish aspects of its reception |
| Learning | Stylistic probability relations and listener dispositions | Schematic, veridical, dynamic and conscious expectations can coexist |
| Limit | An interpretation of tendency does not demonstrate one listener's feeling | A proposed mechanism does not demonstrate artistic value or individual liking |

The rows establish points of comparison, not a translation key. Meyer's three meanings are not Huron's five response systems, and Huron should not be presented as simply experimentally proving Meyer. Use the dedicated Huron reference for qualifications on its mechanisms.

## Worked Example

**Beethoven, Symphony No. 6, first movement, measures 16–26** (chapter IV, Example 44). Meyer uses a motive repeated ten times, with limited dynamic and orchestral alteration, to explain saturation. A listener who expects development can begin to expect change more strongly as the motive persists. The mechanism depends on the context and the understood purpose of repetition; Meyer explicitly contrasts repetition at the end of a work, where lack of forward motion can be appropriate.

Separate the steps: repeated motive is the description; intensified expectation of change is the theoretical interpretation; a particular listener's response remains to be checked; whether the repetition serves the movement is a critical question. In a pop loop, test the role of groove, vocal changes and production before assuming the same expectation of developmental continuation.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- State the expected event or class of events, its basis and the relevant listener competence before invoking inhibition.
- Keep affect, named emotion, connotation, liking and aesthetic value separate.
- Describe how a later event revises an earlier one; do not report the final analytical view as everything a first-time listener knew in advance.
- Preserve saturation's exception for meaningful repetition. Regularity can sustain action or attention without promising immediate change.
- Include performance and context in the causal hypothesis; a score is incomplete evidence for a realized response.
- Use Huron as a distinct model and Hennion/DeNora to investigate practices and situations. None alone supplies a complete account of an individual encounter.

## Key Takeaways

Expectation links musical relations to possible experience through learning, context and time. It can make an interpretation more precise, but it cannot substitute for historical evidence, a listener's report or an argued aesthetic criterion.
