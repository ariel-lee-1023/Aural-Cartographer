## Mental Model (read first)
Genres are historical relations among sounds, names, institutions and social identifications. A category can organize listening and commerce without containing a uniform sound or a uniform population. Trace how distinctions are made, repeated and changed; do not let later labels silently explain earlier music.

## Frameworks & Structure
### 1. Introduction: relations, scale and identification
**Relational genre:** a category becomes intelligible partly through its differences from other categories. Describe the field in which a name operates and the alternatives it excludes. **Scale or level** matters: a broad marketing category and a specific style are not interchangeable units.

**Iterability / citationality** explains how conventions become recognizable through repeated enactment. A genre can be evoked outside its customary setting because signs of it have become legible. This does not mean every genre performance is a literal quotation. Ask which conventions are repeated, who recognizes them, and whether the iteration reinforces or alters the category.

**Music/identification** concerns how musical and social categories become linked. **Homology** proposes a correspondence between social and musical categories; **articulation** emphasizes that the link must be produced and maintained through practices rather than given biologically. The same people may identify with several musics, and a music may enable identifications beyond its presumed constituency. Avoid reading identity directly from a sound.

**Charting popularity** treats trade charts and their accompanying discourse as institutional evidence. They measure according to particular methods and also participate in marketing. Keep the recorded numbers, the category rules and the interpretation of both distinct. *Locator: ch. 1, including “What Is a Popular Music Genre?,” “Music/Identification,” and “Charting Popularity.”*

### 2. Foreign Music and the Emergence of Phonography
“Foreign” organized an early recording market across shifting racial, ethnic, linguistic and national assumptions. It did not simply identify an objective sonic property. Production and cataloguing helped make imagined collectivities available to listeners.

When cataloguing a historical release, retain a period label as evidence of its classification while explaining who used it and what it meant there. Do not silently endorse the category's racial assumptions or replace it with a present-day world-music label. A renamed category can alter what is visible without changing every recording inside it. *Locator: ch. 2.*

### 3. Forward to the Past: Race Music in the 1920s
The success of a recording such as Mamie Smith's “Crazy Blues” participates in an origin narrative, but a category's emergence includes earlier repertories, institutional decisions and later retrospective selection. A market category can consolidate a connection between recordings and an imagined audience.

Distinguish an early commercial breakthrough from the origin of a musical practice. Ask what had to exist before the breakthrough and which histories become invisible when one record is made the beginning. Historical racial terms should be identified as the categories being analyzed. *Locator: ch. 3.*

### 4. The Newness of Old-Time Music
A claim to tradition can be newly organized by recording, naming and marketing. “Old-time,” “hillbilly” and related names did not merely record a timeless rural essence. Brackett traces a field of competing designations and relationships to other market categories.

For a genealogy, separate age of a practice, date of a recorded example, emergence of a category and later authentication of a tradition. A newly commercialized tradition is neither automatically fraudulent nor historically unchanged. *Locator: ch. 4.*

### 5. From Jazz to Pop: Swing in the 1940s
The relationship among jazz, mainstream popularity and racial identification changed through institutions, circulation and sonic practices. A history organized around canonical jazz works can differ from a history of the popular field in which those recordings circulated.

Comparisons of versions, including “Tuxedo Junction,” connect arrangement and recording particulars to the movement of music across categories. Sharing a song does not erase differences between recordings; a cover's circulation does not by itself establish a homogeneous audience. Track both the sonic object and the classification system. *Locator: ch. 5.*

### 6. The Corny-ness of the Folk
Country-related music and race music faced some similar institutional barriers but occupied different positions in relation to the mainstream. Brackett's treatment of “Smoke! Smoke! Smoke! (That Cigarette)” examines a crossover in that changing field.

“Corny” is an evaluative classification with social implications, not a sufficient musical explanation. Ask whether it identifies a performance convention, a relation to a presumed audience, or a devalued social identity. Preserve differences between histories instead of forcing every crossover into one template. *Locator: ch. 6.*

### 7. The Dictionary of Soul
The disappearance of Billboard's R&B chart in 1963 and its return in 1965 cannot be read as the disappearance and return of the music or its audience. Other discourses and classifications continued. The case exposes how an institutional record can change while the field exceeds it.

When archival data has a gap, investigate the publication's category decision and consult other contemporary evidence. A later compilation can make heterogeneous categories appear continuous. Moving from R&B to soul also involves shifts in social identification, rather than a simple replacement of one neutral label by another. *Locator: ch. 7.*

### 8. Crossover Dreams: from Urban Cowboy to the King of Pop
**Crossover** presupposes a boundary. Establish which category a work is leaving, which it is entering, how success is measured and who recognizes the movement. A sound can change, an audience can expand, a chart rule can change, or a performer can be recoded; these are not equivalent events.

The *Urban Cowboy* discussion makes class, gender and country substyles visible together. The Michael Jackson discussion belongs to a changing racial and mainstream category system. Do not use commercial breadth as proof that social boundaries have disappeared. Nor does a cross-chart hit imply that every listener heard it under the same genre. *Locator: ch. 8.*

### 9. Notes Toward a Conclusion
Media formats, visual representations and new ways of tracking popularity change the evidence through which categories are known. The methodological lesson survives particular technologies: study classification as an ongoing practice. Contemporary streaming categories need present evidence; this history does not specify their current operation. *Locator: ch. 9.*

## Worked Example
If an archive shows no Billboard R&B chart during the 1963–65 gap, a naive database might infer no R&B hits or a fully integrated market. Brackett places the chart beside other publications, radio and public discourse. The corrected conclusion is about a classification decision requiring historical explanation, not the extinction of an audience. For the record librarian, preserve the gap and its institutional cause; do not fill it with invented ranks or reinterpret it as musical absence. *Locator: ch. 7, “The Disappearance” and “The Reappearance.”*

## Decision Rules & Judgment
- When classifying a recording, record label, date, classifier and level of category.
- When two works sound similar, separate structural analogy from documented historical relation.
- When an origin story starts at a famous hit, inspect the prehistory and the retrospective selection.
- When a genre is linked to identity, ask how the link is articulated and where it fails.
- When chart data changes, examine measurement and naming before concluding that taste changed.
- When calling a release a crossover, name both sides of the boundary and the evidence of crossing.
- When borrowing a historical racial category, explain its historical usage rather than treating it as a natural kind.

## Key Takeaways
1. Genres are relational, historical and institutionally maintained.
2. Sonic resemblance, identity and market classification can diverge.
3. Archives and charts actively shape the histories they appear merely to record.
4. Crossover is a question about boundaries and power.
5. Cataloguing needs provenance for categories as well as for recordings.
