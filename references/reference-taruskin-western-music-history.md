## Mental Model (read first)

Explain why practices and musical relations became possible and important, rather than narrating a parade of masterpieces. Taruskin combines technical explication with institutions, documents, politics, audiences and the histories people tell about music. His subject is principally the Western literate tradition, not all music made in Europe or the world. His arguments are strong interpretations with identifiable evidence and opponents, not a neutral final court of history.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Historical method: Evidence, causation and narrative

**Literacy** gives the corpus a common feature without making its genres one natural or timeless category. Writing changes musical practice and also changes what historians can know. The beginning of surviving notation is not the beginning of music, polyphony, instruments or secular practice. Selection by documentary survival must remain visible.

**Technical explication and causal explanation** answer different questions. A progression can be described internally while the conditions of its composition, circulation or reception require other evidence. Avoid both an autonomous score-history and an account in which politics mechanically dictates every note.

**Poietic / esthesic:** distinguish making and makers' purposes from perception, audience expectations and use. Taruskin's early-opera discussion explicitly widens an origin story centered on composers and theorists to include patrons and audiences. Do not conflate “esthesic” with an aesthetic verdict.

**Inclusion is not a value ranking:** the introduction states that historical explanatory purpose governs selection, not the worth of every included or omitted work. Equally, a constructed canon can contain substantial achievements; explaining its institutions does not render all judgments meaningless. Source: general introduction and volume 2, chapter 1.

### Volume 1 — Music from the Earliest Notations to the Sixteenth Century

**Route:** 19 chapters move from chant and literacy through theories of pitch, secular traditions, polyphony, Notre Dame, Ars Nova, Machaut, regional exchanges, dynastic patronage, humanism, print, confessional conflict and radical humanist pressures. This route locates further reading; not every technical system in those chapters is reproduced here.

**Chant and literacy:** the surviving sacred, liturgical, monophonic corpus cannot establish that other musical activities did not exist. In “The Curtain Goes Up,” Taruskin links transmission and writing to political and ecclesiastical circumstances rather than an invention of music ex nihilo. For an origin claim, distinguish the first surviving witness, a documented practice and the unknown earlier history. Locator: volume 1, chapter 1, “Literacy.”

**Theory and practice:** modal and contrapuntal categories belong to historically changing practices. Do not translate a medieval mode into a modern major/minor key simply because some notes coincide. Technical claims need the relevant period's resources; Laitz's common-practice toolkit is not sufficient to adjudicate every early notation or theory.

**Emulation, patronage and print:** musical relationships can be acts of homage and competitive display within an institutional tradition. Taruskin's account of the *L'Homme armé* mass lineage connects Josquin's treatment to earlier composers and to Petrucci's 1502 printed volume of his masses. Printing and single-composer presentation change circulation and stature as well as preserve notes. Locator: volume 1, chapter 12, concluding Josquin/Petrucci discussion; chapter 13 supplies the larger print route.

**Reformation and humanist routes:** use chapters 14–19 to investigate changing relationships among text, intelligibility, musical craft, confessional institutions and publics. Claims about a particular council, doctrine or composer's response require direct chapter/document verification. Do not adopt a one-cause tale in which textual expressiveness inevitably produces opera.

**Cross-round use:** Katz asks how recording changes practice; this volume supplies a historically different case of a medium changing practice and evidence. Similar explanatory questions do not make notation and phonography equivalent technologies.

### Volume 2 — Music in the Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries

**Route:** 13 chapters connect early opera, courts and political change, opera seria, concerto/tonality-driven form, Bach and Handel, comic style, Enlightenment reform, instrumental prestige, the composer's voice and Beethoven-era transformations.

**Opera and its politics:** *Orfeo* and *Poppea* do not belong to identical producing and receiving situations simply because both are by Monteverdi. Ask who pays, who attends, what occasion is served and how theatrical possibilities change. The court spectacle's praise of a patron is part of its function. Explaining the social costs of patronage is distinct from evaluating the resulting work. Locator: volume 2, chapter 1, “Opera and Its Politics.”

**Class and classicism:** the Naples discussion relates conservatories' origins as institutions housing and training children to the conditions of musical labor and operatic production. “Conservatory” does not always name the same institution across centuries. Do not infer musical abundance directly from a prosperous or humane society. Locator: volume 2, chapter 4, “Naples.”

**Tonality-driven form:** treat the rise of new tonal organizations as a historical question about genres, practices and institutions, not the discovery of a universal natural law. Chapters 5–10 provide the path from concerto practice and tonal procedure toward new instrumental roles. Laitz explains local syntax; Caplin explains a more narrowly delimited later Classical repertoire.

**Canon and voice:** later audiences and critics can hear autonomy or personal revelation in works whose original occasions and functions were different. Track when such a hearing becomes influential. Rosen's close account of Classical dramatic syntax and Taruskin's institutional history can illuminate the same work while making different claims.

### Volume 3 — Music in the Nineteenth Century

**Route:** 14 chapters include Romantic consciousness, character pieces and song, national identification, virtuosity and criticism, opera, nationalism, changing forms and the return/international spread of the symphony.

**The music trance:** an account of subjective consciousness and musical absorption helps explain a new way of valuing experience. It is a historical aesthetic, not a timeless description of all listening. Ask which spaces, instruments and practices sustain it; pair with Hennion and DeNora to investigate the actual encounter. Locator: volume 3, chapter 2, “The I and the We.”

**Conservatories, textbook forms and the work-concept:** retrospective codification helps establish a Classical period and repertoire as standards to preserve. The chapter on virtuosos relates sonata-form textbooks and standardized education to the distinction between an avant-garde and an establishment. Do not assume a later textbook diagram was the composer's own explicit rule. Locator: volume 3, chapter 5, discussion of Reicha, Marx and canonization.

**Nationalism's asymmetry:** a dominant nation's conventions can be heard as universal while another nation's music is marked as national. Taruskin's discussion of Schumann receiving Chopin exposes this uneven labeling. Separate nationality, state citizenship, claimed identity, musical sign and reception. A folk-like trait is not sufficient proof of provenance. Locator: volume 3, chapter 7, opening Chopin discussion.

**Museum culture:** the symphony's renewed prestige is connected to public concert life, halls, orchestras, associations and preserved repertoire, not explained by the arrival of talented composers alone. A canon has institutions that repeat and authorize it. Map those mechanisms alongside the musical achievement; do not replace one with the other. Locator: volume 3, chapter 13, “Museum Culture.”

**Cross-round use:** Brackett studies relational categories and their changing audiences; Wald asks what later canons erase. Apply both questions to art music without assuming that concert canonization and popular chart categories operate identically.

### Volume 4 — Music in the Early Twentieth Century

**Route:** 13 chapters organize competing modernisms through limits, discontinuity, maximalism, transcendental claims, nationalism, neoclassicism, the commonplace, American identities, utopian projects and totalitarian society.

**Maximalism:** intensifying inherited resources can involve extension, compression, expressive pressure and competition. It is not synonymous with “large orchestra,” nor simply an inevitable path toward atonality. In “Reaching (for) Limits,” increasingly dense sonorities can retain contextual connections even when a conventional chord name becomes inadequate.

**Harmony as contextual action:** Taruskin's Strauss *Rêverie* discussion describes a chromatic-neighbor collection by what it does over a tonic pedal and how it returns. An unusual verticality can have clear immediate voice-leading while larger coherence requires another account. Use this as a question for Laitz/Straus, not as permission to force one chord label across the whole repertoire. Locator: volume 4, chapter 1, example 1-8 and surrounding discussion.

**Plural modernisms:** the volume's chapter architecture refuses to make all difference equivalent to one emancipatory tonal history. Specify a composer's technique, rhetoric, institution and contemporaries. A common date or dissonant sound does not establish shared aims or documented influence.

**Politics and musical action:** locate relevant primary documents before deciding what a work did under a regime or how an artist negotiated it. Musical structure cannot, by itself, certify resistance, compliance or moral innocence. Volume 4, chapter 13 is a research route; detailed disputed cases need consultation beyond this condensed reference.

### Volume 5 — Music in the Late Twentieth Century

**Route:** 10 chapters move through postwar reconstruction, indeterminacy and electronics, Cold War conflicts, blurred categories, minimalism, lost consensuses and the end of the millennium.

**Starting from scratch:** zero-hour rhetoric is itself part of history. Institutions and authority help determine which innovations count. Taruskin's Darmstadt discussion distinguishes claimed Webernian lineage from the actual procedures of new works; he also identifies Messiaen's *Mode de valeurs et d'intensités* as a consequential case without calling it a twelve-tone row piece. Claimed ancestry, technical similarity and institutional endorsement require different evidence. Locator: volume 5, chapter 1, “Fixations” and preceding discussion.

**Cold War interpretation:** connect aesthetic commitments to their institutional and political settings without treating those settings as a complete explanation of the music. The author himself qualifies an account of authoritarian rhetoric as revealing but not all-explaining. Political context is a research obligation, not a shortcut to every conclusion.

**Blurred sociostylistic categories:** discussion of Berio and rock shows how admiration can coexist with paternalistic ideas of another music's purity or naturalness. Amplification can be an instrument of construction rather than a neutral extension. Pair with Zak, Frith and Wilson: ask what the recording does and what the critical vocabulary assumes about its makers and listeners. Locator: volume 5, chapter 7, Berio/Beatles discussion.

**Minimalism / pattern and process music:** the label covers divergent technical practices and crosses classical/popular boundaries. Commercial success and avant-garde status can coexist uneasily; neither settles the musical evaluation. Specify process, ensemble and listening relation instead of defining the category solely by repetition or duration. Locator: volume 5, chapter 8, “New Sites of Innovation.”

**Postmodernism and listening:** challenges to novelty as the sole criterion of progress open questions about listener intelligibility and social relation. Taruskin's discussion of Lerdahl distinguishes complexity from unprocessable surface complicatedness as part of that debate. This is not a license to judge every work by immediate accessibility or predictive ease. Locator: volume 5, chapter 9, discussion before “The End of Soviet Music.”

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- For a historical claim, identify period, place, institution, actors, evidence and the mechanism connecting them. Fill neither dates nor transmission gaps by resemblance.
- Separate source survival from historical existence; technical novelty from importance; canonization from merit; contemporary reception from later reputation.
- Ask whose categories are being used and when they became authoritative. “Classical,” “national,” “modern” and “popular” have histories.
- Pair technical relations with conditions of production and reception. Neither side should erase the other.
- Attribute Taruskin's interpretations and polemics; compare them with Rosen, Ross and documentary evidence where conclusions diverge.
- Use the chapter routes to locate deeper evidence. This one reference is intentionally selective and cannot answer every detailed question across five volumes.
- Keep the archive on demand: load this reference for a historical problem, not to impose a large Western timeline on every musical encounter.

## Key Takeaways

Musical languages, institutions and narratives are made together through contingent histories. Genealogy requires transmission evidence; analogy can remain useful without pretending to be genealogy. Historical understanding should widen criticism and discovery without converting the canon into a universal map of music.
