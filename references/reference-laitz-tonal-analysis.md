## Mental Model (read first)

Tonal analysis joins melody, counterpoint, harmony, rhythm and hearing. A chord inventory is a first level of description; a second level asks which events prolong a harmony, connect structural harmonies, articulate a cadence or establish a new key. The same vertical collection can do different work in different contexts. Laitz supplies a common-practice foundation, not a universal grammar or a quality standard for every repertoire.

## Frameworks & Structure

### Parts 1–2, chapters 1–8: From musical space and time to tonal motion

- **Pitch, scale degree and interval:** distinguish sounding pitch, letter spelling, octave/register and scale-relative role. Establish a plausible local tonic and mode before assigning Roman numerals. An accidental can express a tendency or a local tonicization; it does not by itself establish a new key. Preserve enharmonic spelling when voice-leading or key relations matter.
- **Pulse, rhythm and meter:** pulse supplies a reference beat; meter groups beats hierarchically; rhythm distributes durations and attacks. A notated barline is evidence for a metric reading, not proof of every performed accent. Track displacement, syncopation and grouping separately from tempo or harmonic rhythm. Changing note density need not accelerate the harmonic rhythm.
- **Melody and two-voice counterpoint:** hear a line's contour, leaps, stepwise continuations and points of arrival, then inspect the intervals it makes against another line. Contrary, similar, parallel and oblique motion describe relations between voices, not character or quality. A voice-leading account explains why a verticality occurs when chord spelling alone does not.
- **Triads, seventh chords and inversion:** determine chord quality/root independently of the bass note. Figured bass specifies intervals above the bass; it does not determine harmonic function. Separate a change of inversion from a change of harmony. Common-practice part-writing constraints train an idiom; their violation in another idiom does not establish bad composition.
- **Tonic and dominant as tonal pillars:** tonic provides a point of reference; dominant acquires directional significance in a tonal context. Identify tendency tones and their actual continuations, particularly leading tone and chordal seventh. A dominant-shaped sonority can be a local color or a prolonging event rather than the phrase's structural dominant.
- **Reduction and elaboration:** move between the sounding surface and a proposed simpler voice-leading continuity. Retain bass, melody, metric placement and phrase goals that justify omissions. A reduction is a claim about relationships at a chosen level, not a transcription of everything important in the experience. Do not erase a salient event merely to obtain a tidy progression.

### Part 3, chapters 9–12: The phrase model and embellishment

- **Harmonic phrase model:** tonic → pre-dominant → dominant → tonic is a governing pattern for this repertoire. Phases can be expanded, embedded, omitted or redirected. Label individual chords first, then show which larger function they sustain. Distinguish a progression across functions from motion within one prolonged function.
- **Pre-dominant:** ii/ii° and IV/iv commonly prepare dominant harmony. In minor, the first-inversion supertonic avoids the instability of its root-position diminished triad. This is a contextual role: an IV used to prolong tonic is not automatically an independent pre-dominant phase.
- **Cadence:** locate an ending within a phrase, not merely an adjacent pair of chords. A perfect authentic cadence requires root-position dominant and tonic with tonic in the upper voice at the arrival; other authentic closures require more specific qualification. A half cadence ends on dominant; a deceptive arrival redirects the expected tonic. Explain the degree and level of closure instead of treating cadence names as interchangeable.
- **Phrygian half cadence:** in minor, iv6–V gives the bass a semitone descent, scale degrees 6–5, toward a dominant goal. Its name concerns this cadential bass relation, not the passage's membership in a Phrygian mode. Source: chapter 9, pp. 265–267.
- **Embellishing tones:** distinguish passing and neighboring motion, suspension, appoggiatura, anticipation and pedal by preparation, attack, metric position and resolution. A suspension retains a prepared note against a changed harmony before resolving; an accented dissonance without that preparation needs another account. Identify the sustained or moving voices, not just a label attached to a note.
- **Six-four chords:** separate passing, pedal/neighboring, arpeggiating and cadential uses. A cadential six-four generally elaborates dominant through accented 6–5 and 4–3 motion over the dominant bass; its tonic-triad pitch content does not make it a stable tonic arrival. Arpeggiating six-fours can belong to one sustained root-position harmony rather than constitute new functional chords. Source: chapter 11, especially pp. 303–311.
- **Phrase extension and embedded models:** distinguish the local completion from the larger progression in which it participates. An interior cadence can be real without ending the whole theme; conversely, prolongation can make many surface chords serve one phase.

### Part 4, chapters 13–17: More than a chain of chord roots

- **Submediant and mediant:** vi/VI may bridge tonic and pre-dominant, substitute for an expected tonic or participate in a sequence; iii/III can participate in arpeggiation and fifth-related progressions. Determine role from the whole progression. Do not infer deceptive cadence from every V–vi motion.
- **Back-relating dominant (BRD):** a dominant can prolong a preceding tonic without resolving to a following tonic. Laitz's Bach example makes the later pre-dominant and structural dominant decisive: treating the earlier V as structural would obscure that trajectory. He extends the idea to a half-cadence boundary at a deeper level. Preserve the analytical level; do not use BRD to declare that any V before ii is irrelevant. Source: chapter 14, pp. 372–374.
- **Period:** coordinate melodic comparison with harmonic contrast between an antecedent and a more conclusive consequent. State cadence types and keys before choosing a period label. Similar melodic openings alone do not prove a period.
- **Sentence, double period and modified period:** phrase length and repetition patterns provide clues, while continuation and closure determine how those clues work. These are specific tonal designs, not the only viable organizations of musical time. For the stricter Classical formal-function account, use Caplin rather than silently mixing terminology.
- **Harmonic sequence:** identify a model and the interval/rhythm of its repetitions; distinguish a diatonic sequence, which adjusts interval quality to the key, from chromatic versions. State the span and destination. Sequential coherence can temporarily outrank local chord-by-chord functionality, but its entrance and exit still matter.

### Parts 5–6, chapters 18–24: Chromaticism with a destination

- **Applied chords:** V/x or vii°/x locally intensifies a diatonic target. Verify the target and voice-leading; an altered pitch is insufficient. Separate tonicization within an ongoing home-key phrase from modulation that gives a new key stronger cadential and subsequent confirmation.
- **Tonicization versus modulation:** inspect cadence strength, duration, continued activity in the new key and the surrounding hierarchy. Laitz treats the boundary as contextual, not a fixed number of bars. A pivot chord can carry two key-relative interpretations, but direct and other modulations need not have one. Source: chapter 19, pp. 467–470.
- **Binary form and variations:** distinguish the tonal trajectory and return strategy from surface A/B lettering. In variation, identify what persists—bass, harmonic route, phrase design, tune—and what changes. A recognizable melody need not be the invariant in every variation.
- **Modal mixture:** borrowing from a parallel mode changes scale degrees and harmonic color while potentially preserving tonic. Distinguish mixture from a change to the relative key and from an applied dominant. In songs, read the relation to words and phrase position without equating minor with a fixed emotion.
- **Chromatic modulation:** inspect common tones, enharmonic reinterpretation and the degree to which a new tonal area is confirmed. A pitch held across the boundary can change role even while the sounding note stays constant.
- **Neapolitan (♭II):** often a first-inversion pre-dominant, but also capable of root-position statement, prolongation, tonicization and sequence. Laitz's Beethoven Op. 59 No. 2 example embeds a strongly asserted ♭II within a larger i–♭II–V–i motion. Its special color and its larger function are compatible. Source: chapter 23, pp. 554–561.
- **Augmented sixth:** identify the defining outward semitone motions, ♭6 down to 5 and ♯4 up to 5, rather than mistaking enharmonic resemblance for dominant-seventh function. Italian adds tonic; French also adds scale degree 2; German also adds lowered 3 in major/the diatonic third in minor. German-to-V can produce parallel fifths; an intervening cadential six-four commonly changes the voice-leading. These are idiom-specific procedures, not prohibitions on other musical styles. Source: chapter 24, pp. 569–573.

### Parts 7–9, chapters 25–32: Large form and the limits of the tonal foundation

- **Ternary, rondo and sonata:** identify return, contrast, tonal plan and the transformations that make a return significant. A sonata exposition establishes a tonal departure; recapitulation changes the tonal consequences of returning material. Merely finding two tunes or A–B–A is inadequate.
- **Nineteenth-century harmonic tendencies:** third-related tonal paths, augmented-triad/diminished-seventh symmetries and chromatic sequences can weaken the directional asymmetries of ordinary diatonic relations. Explain the particular progression; do not turn the textbook's historical trajectory into an inevitable collapse of tonality. Symmetric pitch resources can still support a center through register, duration or emphasis.
- **Twentieth-/twenty-first-century resources:** vestiges of common practice coexist with noncentric, atonal, rhythmic and ordered pitch-class procedures. Stop importing tonic/dominant roles when the evidence no longer supports them. Use Straus for post-tonal operations and keep pitch organization distinct from rhythmic, timbral or studio organization.

## Worked Example

**Tonicization is not automatically modulation.** In Example 19.6, pp. 467–469, Laitz contrasts Beethoven's Violin Sonata in A, Op. 12 No. 2, with Haydn's E-minor Piano Sonata Hob. XVI:34. His Beethoven reading places local tonicizations inside the larger I–ii–IV–V–I progression; the strong cadence comes at the end of the excerpt. In the Haydn, a PAC in G at measures 7–8 and continuation there at measure 9 support a temporarily established new key. The larger E-minor relation remains intelligible.

The procedure is to hear/inspect the bass and melody, locate cadential confirmation, test what follows, then distinguish the local key from the larger tonal plan. This source example does not license declaring every long tonicization a modulation. In a recorded song, use the same question about local versus global organization, then check whether groove, phrase placement or production gives the event a salience that a harmonic reduction omits.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Establish the passage, evidence and stylistic hypothesis before using functional labels. Keep ambiguous bass notes and missing accidentals unresolved until verified.
- Separate chord identity, function, level and phrase position. Repeated V–I is not automatically repeated structural closure.
- For “more tense,” identify the changed tendency tones, dissonances, bass direction, harmonic rhythm, register or delayed goal. Treat the predicted effect as a hypothesis for a style-competent listener.
- Compare at least two levels when a local event conflicts with a global reading. Preserve a rival analysis if it explains a salient relation the first misses.
- Common-practice voice-leading rules explain conventions. They cannot alone evaluate modal loops, blues syntax, non-Western traditions, post-tonal music or production-led form.
- Return from reduction to realization: instrumentation, voicing, attack, intonation, groove and recording can determine what the listener notices.

## Key Takeaways

Tonal precision comes from relating vertical events to moving voices and temporal goals. A good analysis explains which events carry structural weight and why, while keeping the style hypothesis, expressive interpretation and aesthetic verdict independently revisable.
